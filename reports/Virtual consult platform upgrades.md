# Make the follow-up plan a real document

The single highest-value change to SlickChart's virtual consult is not a new capability — it is giving the thing she already sends a structure. Every line of evidence in this research converges on the written follow-up as the whole product: it is what the client is promised, the unpaid labor the provider performs after the review, the artifact that determines whether the client believes she was actually examined, the artifact that carries her product revenue, and the artifact where scope-of-practice exposure lives. Today SlickChart delivers it as one free-text textarea pasted into a chat bubble, which is the worst possible container for it. The health-communication literature is unusually specific about what the container should do — resolve every instruction to a named time slot, a named amount and named days, because comprehension of "at 7 AM and 7 PM" measured **89%** against **53%** for "every 12 hours" ([AHRQ](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/pharmhealthlit/prescriptionmed-instr.html), search-summary sourced, unverified) — and the acne adherence literature says the follow-up cadence should be built around **week 6**, where measured adherence collapsed from **86% at week 1 to 36%** ([Patient Prefer Adherence](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.2147/PPA.S117437)), not around the 12-week efficacy horizon the clinical guidelines use. Of the three specific provider requests, two are cheap and should ship first: editable invite wording is a small, well-understood feature with a clear borrowed design, and "the follow-up documents need more structure" is the central recommendation of this report. Two-way Google Calendar sync is real, wanted, and the single largest engineering item here — do it, but do it last and do only half of it.

**A standing caveat on sourcing.** The research environment's egress proxy blocked first-hand reads of PubMed/PMC, AHRQ, AAD/JAAD, ASCP, AmSpa, HHS/OCR, Reddit, GoHighLevel's own docs and help center, Capterra, G2, and essentially every vendor pricing and help page. Most findings below are search-engine paraphrases of those pages rather than full-text reads, and are labeled where that matters. **No competitor or vendor pricing appears in this report at all**, because the figures encountered were third-party and mutually contradictory. Several circulating numbers were explicitly excluded by the notes as unusable (vendor no-show-cost and added-revenue claims, GHL review scores, content-farm skincare ramping guides) and are not repeated here. Where a proposed structure is a construction by the researchers rather than a published standard, it says so.

## The three requests, answered

### Google Calendar: build the read side, not two-way sync

**What exists:** a one-way ICS subscription feed, surfaced in Settings, that makes SlickChart appointments appear in Google or Apple Calendar. That covers the "see my work in my phone" half and should be left alone.

**What is missing is the only half providers actually buy.** The feature as marketed by the two platforms that document it properly is not "sync" — it is *your personal calendar's busy time blocks client bookings*. Practice Better describes Google Calendar syncing "in near real time, so busy events automatically block client bookings" ([Practice Better](https://practicebetter.io/integrations/google-calendar)); IntakeQ documents a two-way sync where "the widget reads the external calendar to determine availability before showing open spots" ([IntakeQ support](https://support.intakeq.com/article/163-sync-with-google-calendar)). Both reads are search-summary, not first-hand. Jane App is **one-way only and reviewers name it as a con** ([Software Advice](https://www.softwareadvice.com/compare/81975-simplepractice/vs/86099-Jane-App/)), and athenahealth has no native two-way sync at all, with the failure described precisely: a clinician "glancing at their personal Google Calendar sees an open block and accepts a meeting that athenahealth had already filled" ([Sporo Health](https://info.sporo.health/athenahealth-to-google-calendar-appointment-sync/)).

The mechanism of SlickChart's current limitation is documented and is not a bug in our implementation: **ICS feeds are read-only, and Google refreshes them only a few times a day, creating a 3-to-24-hour window in which the two systems disagree** ([Upheal](https://www.upheal.io/blog/therapy-calenar-sync-how-to-fix-booking-gaps)). The same source reports a therapist double-booked three times in one week from exactly this drift. A virtual consult makes the collision worse than an in-studio appointment does, because it can be taken from anywhere at odd hours ([SpaSphere](https://spasphere.ai/blog/best-esthetician-booking-software) — vendor blog, weak source, cited only for the stated motive "keeping personal and work calendars in one view").

**Recommendation: implement the inbound direction only.** Google OAuth with a calendar scope, read the provider's chosen calendars (free/busy is enough — we never need event titles, which is also the privacy-minimising choice), subtract those busy blocks from her SlickChart availability, and keep pushing outbound via the existing ICS feed. That gets the whole marketed benefit without owning bidirectional write semantics. GoHighLevel's experience is the argument for stopping there: its reschedule flow **deletes the original appointment and creates a new one, re-firing "Appointment Booked" without firing "Appointment Cancelled"** and breaking downstream logic ([GHL Scale Up](https://www.ghlscaleup.com/blog/gohighlevel-appointment-workflow-not-triggering)), corroborated by an open request on HighLevel's own ideas board for a native "Appointment Rescheduled" trigger ([ideas.gohighlevel.com](https://ideas.gohighlevel.com/scheduling-calendar/p/add-native-appointment-rescheduled-trigger-or-identifier-for-workflows)).

Two non-negotiables come with it. First, **every reported sync failure in this research is a silent one** — a revoked OAuth token, a calendar not included in conflict checking, a permissions error. Practice Better maintains a dedicated troubleshooting article whose first step is disconnect-and-reconnect ([Practice Better](https://help.practicebetter.io/hc/en-us/articles/360029959431-Google-Calendar-Sync-Troubleshooting)), and GoHighLevel users report Google silently blocking the connection after password changes or suspicious-login detection ([ConsultEvo](https://consultevo.com/gohighlevel-google-calendar-reintegration/)). So a visible connection-health state, a last-synced timestamp, and one-click reconnect are part of the feature, not polish. Second, this stores a **third-party OAuth token per provider**, which is precisely the asset class behind the shared-Square-token incident in CLAUDE.md §0: the tokens belong in a per-owner table shaped like `square_connections`, resolved only from the verified session, with no fallback path that could ever let one account reach another's calendar. Offline tolerance argues for caching the busy snapshot with its staleness shown rather than failing the availability screen when Google is unreachable.

**Size: large.** Lowest value-to-effort of the three requests, and the only one with an ongoing operational tail.

### Editable invite wording: copy GoHighLevel's custom-value split

**What exists:** one hardcoded sentence with an emoji, identical for every provider and every consult type.

**The demand is real but inferred, not stated.** No practitioner was found asking a software tool for tone control — Reddit, ASCP and the trade press were all blocked. What is observable is a live commercial market for esthetician email templates sold explicitly on voice: templates "written in a spa-toned, high-end voice so messages feel polished, warm, and on-brand," pitched as "estheticians don't need to sound like a copywriter — they just need to sound like the expert their clients already trust" ([Esthetician Training](https://estheticiantraining.biz/pages/esthetician-email-templates-that-book-free-kit), search-summary). People are paying for pre-written client emails, and the thing being sold is that they will not sound pre-written. The negative-space evidence is just as good: the most-criticized AI feature in this category is faulted because **"you can't adjust style, tone, or structure"** ([Twofold](https://www.trytwofold.com/compare/simplepractice-ai-note-taker-review) — competitor commentary, not disinterested).

**The holistic/clinical split the request names is a declared professional identity, not a tone slider.** A holistic esthetician "views the skin as inseparable from the body, the mind, and spirit" and "gathers information not only about your skin, but also about your diet, lifestyle, and physical and emotional wellness," producing a plan addressing "ALL pillars of skin health" ([Vibrant Skin Bar](https://vibrantskinbar.com/blog/holistic-esthetician/); [Lavender & Thyme](https://www.lavenderandthymeaesthetics.com/blog/whatisaholisticaesthetician), both search-summary). It is a trained specialization with its own certificate program ([LearnSkin](https://get.learnskin.com/iecp/)). That changes *which sections the plan has*, not only its adjectives — so word-level customization alone will be rejected by the half of the market it does not fit, and the plan-template work in the next section has to carry the same configurability.

**Recommendation: steal the cleanest idea in GoHighLevel, which is its two-namespace templating.** Per-recipient data lives in custom fields on the contact; per-business constants live in account-level **custom values** the provider sets once — her business name, booking link, policy, shop URL — referenced as merge tokens and resolving in the same namespace everywhere: emails, SMS, forms, workflows ([ConsultEvo](https://consultevo.com/gohighlevel-custom-values-guide/); [Growthable](https://growthable.io/gohighlevel-tutorials/campaigns/overview-of-merge-fields-custom-variables-in-gohighlevel/), reseller doc-mirrors). She edits the constant in one place and every message she has ever written follows. Without it, a provider who changes her booking URL has to hunt every template she owns, will not, and her messages quietly go stale. GoHighLevel's exact token syntax could not be verified — third-party sources conflict between `{{ contact.first_name }}` and `{first_name}` — so pick one and document it rather than copying a guess.

Also worth copying: the distinction between a **template** (the body of an automated send) and a **snippet** (a fragment she drops into a reply she is writing now) ([ConsultEvo](https://consultevo.com/gohighlevel-text-snippets-guide/)). They share storage and the same resolver, so supporting both is nearly free and covers two genuinely different moments. Default to editable copy that ships already written: the niche-"snapshot" market, where beauty businesses pay strangers for pre-wired sequences ([AutoGenCRM](https://autogencrm.com/gohighlevel-snapshot-marketplace/)), is evidence that solo operators will not build this from empty.

Scope this to: one account-level default invite message, optional per-consult-type overrides, a short token set, and a live preview. **Size: small to medium.** Best value-to-effort of the three requests.

### "The follow-up documents need more structure": she is right, and the gap is bigger than wording

**What exists:** one free-text textarea, delivered as a chat message beginning "Your virtual consult review is ready." No sections, no AM/PM layout, no timeline, no follow-up date, no templates, no document view. The product plan and home-care list are separate features that do not travel with the review as one artifact.

**There is no published standard for this document.** No board, association or peer-reviewed source defines a client-facing skincare plan structure; commercial esthetics templates converge only on *intake and charting* fields, which is a different artifact and shipping them to a client would reproduce the known failure of the clinical after-visit summary ([Pabau](https://pabau.com/templates/skin-care-consultation-form/); [Carepatron](https://www.carepatron.com/templates/esthetician-consultation-form) — prevailing practice, not evidence). So **the structure below is the researchers' construction, derived from comprehension and adherence evidence rather than from a standard, and should be presented to Ashley as such.**

What the evidence does support is strong and specific. Patient-facing material should target **6th-to-8th grade reading level**, and in an audit of discharge instructions at a large academic center **only 7% met it** ([SHM Converge abstract](https://education.hospitalmedicine.org/AssetListing/SHM-Converge-Scientific-Abstract-ePoster-Gallery-13901/Barriers-to-Patient-Empowerment-Evaluating-Literacy-Level-of-Discharge-Instructions-27319)). The explicitness gradient is the largest implementable effect: comprehension ran **89% for a precise time of day, 77% for a named period, 61% for "twice a day", 53% for an hourly interval** ([AHRQ](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/pharmhealthlit/prescriptionmed-instr.html), unverified-fetch), "use as directed" is independently identified as a source of confusion ([Pharmacy Times](https://pharmacytimes.com/view/use-as-directed-is-a-source-of-confusion)), and prescribers omit the amount or the timing roughly 42-45% of the time when counseling ([Drug Topics](https://drugtopics.com/view/low-literacy-may-lead-medication-nonadherence)) — meaning **omission is the default failure, so the form must require those fields rather than invite them.** The Universal Medication Schedule's fixed slots (morning, noon, evening, bedtime) map almost exactly onto AM / PM / weekly ([AHRQ](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/pharmacy/instructions.html), unverified-fetch), which argues that **the slot is the primary organizing key and the product is the child record** — a list of products the client must re-sort into a routine is the "every 12 hours" failure mode. Pairing instructions with images has the biggest measured effect in this literature (**85% vs 14%** correct recall of spoken instructions with pictographs, [Houts et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10026551/); **35% more** recalled at discharge in a randomized trial of pictograph-enhanced instructions, though **recall at one week was equal between arms** — [Univ. of Utah / JAMIA](https://collections.lib.utah.edu/details?id=1423161)), so do not over-claim durability.

**The proposed section order — researchers' construction, label it as such:** the goal restated in the client's own words; **what to do tonight**, first, because unprioritized comprehensiveness is the documented AVS failure; the PM routine as a numbered list with explicit amounts and named days; the AM routine; weekly steps; a week-indexed introduction schedule; what to expect and when, including normal temporary worsening; cautions and stop-rules; when to see a doctor as a visually separate block; the next check-in date with the photo retake instructions written explicitly ("same bathroom, morning, before you wash your face"); and the full product list **last**, as reference. Putting the goal and the next action first and the inventory last is the opposite of every intake template found.

Three further constraints come from outside the comprehension literature and matter as much. **Cap the product count.** A practitioner guide warns that "one of the biggest virtual consultation pitfalls is overwhelming clients with too much information" and advises **three to five** key recommendations ([Pomp](https://www.pompbeauty.com/blog/how-to-maximize-virtual-skincare-consultations-a-guide-for-estheticians) — vendor, search-summary); the 2024 AAD guideline's reasoning for fixed-dose combinations is explicitly **to facilitate adherence** ([JAAD](https://www.jaad.org/article/S0190-9622(23)03389-3/fulltext)), which generalizes to a non-prescription plan as fewer products, fewer steps, fewer decisions. **Demonstrate the examination.** The dominant client complaint about skincare consults is that they felt like "sales pitches rather than professional services," with one reviewer describing a practitioner who "barely examined their skin at all" ([Trustpilot](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/skinlaundry.com) — adjacent evidence about an in-person laser chain, not a solo esthetician's virtual consult; weak and partly off-target). The structural answer is to quote the client's own intake answers and product-audit rows back at her — naming what to **keep** from her existing shelf, not only what to buy. The keep/toss step is a trust artifact, not just advice ([Glowday](https://www.glowday.com/blog/how-to-prepare-for-a-virtual-skin-consultation), UK source). And **put the document at a link, not in the chat.** A plan the client must scroll a message thread to find is a plan she stops opening; a stable client-facing page, reachable from the existing magic-link client app, is the fix — with the chat message reduced to a pointer.

## Acne is the best case for asynchronous review, and "not asking" is the documented failure mode

The modality evidence is more favorable than it might feel. Store-and-forward teledermatology achieves roughly **60-90%** diagnostic concordance with in-person assessment, with other studies reporting **79-94%** ([JMIR Dermatology](https://derma.jmir.org/2022/3/e37517)), and **acne sits at the top of that range** — one randomized cohort comparing image-acquisition techniques found acne had the highest diagnostic match across all image types ([Saade et al.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/ijta/5789165)), and a COVID-era comparison found "good conformity with face-to-face consultation in assessing the severity of acne vulgaris" ([JCAD](https://jcadonline.com/acne-severity-teledermatology-covid-pandemic/)). None of these were read in full text. Concordance degrades for pigmented lesions and for anything needing palpation — which is an argument for routing those out, not for distrusting the whole workflow.

The canonical critique is the 2016 JAMA Dermatology secret-shopper study — **62 encounters across 16 direct-to-consumer sites and apps, 6 standardized cases** — which found quality "adequate for simple dermatologic scenarios but poor for scenarios requiring more than basic histories," reported patients **wrongly reassured that a lesion was benign 21% of the time**, and concluded the services "failed to ask simple, relevant questions of patients about their symptoms, leading them to repeatedly miss important diagnoses" ([PM360 summary](https://pm360online.com/study-finds-gaps-in-dtc-teledermatology-quality/)). That figure is **from 2016, predates the COVID teledermatology expansion, and no modern replication was found**; it reaches this report through a trade summary, not the paper. Even discounted, its direction is the most useful design finding available: **the failure mode of consumer skin consults was not misreading, it was not asking.** A form that requires answers beats a free-text box. SlickChart's intake today is a type-driven photo list plus a free-text goals field plus product rows — strong on the shelf audit, thin on history.

The named causes of discordance in clinical practice map one-to-one onto features: poor image quality, inadequate history, inability to evaluate texture, atypical presentations, and **evolution of the condition between capture and read** ([JMIR Dermatology case series](https://derma.jmir.org/2021/1/e24820/)). That last one is why a consult built on photos older than about a week should warn. On photography, the binding published guidance is clinician-era and should be labeled as such — the ATA's store-and-forward figures of **800×600 preferred 1024×768, minimum 75 ppi, 24-bit color** are **2012 numbers that any modern phone exceeds by an order of magnitude** ([ATA Teledermatology Practice Guidelines](https://caltrc.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/ATA-Teledermatology-Practice-Guidelines.pdf)) — while the durable requirements are framing ones: views from **different proximities**, camera perpendicular to the skin, and overview/mid-range/close-up ([Consensus Guidelines for Teledermatology scoping review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10335147/)). The same review notes that **patients struggle** with dermatological photography and that published guidance is written for clinicians. Standardized facial photography conventionally uses five positions — frontal, both laterals, both 45° obliques ([PMC12451680](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12451680/)) — and home capture cannot reproduce research lighting, so the achievable target is **consistency, not standardization**: same room, same time of day, and a ghosted overlay of the baseline shot at capture time, which is how commercial derm-photo tooling solves it ([RxPhoto](https://rxphoto.com/use-cases/dermatologists), vendor). This matters specifically for acne because lighting variation distorts apparent **redness and inflammation** ([Validated Clinical Solutions](https://validatedcs.com/consumer-product-testing/before-and-after-photography/)) — an unstandardized follow-up photo reads as a change in inflammation that is really a change in the lamp. No published guidance was found on makeup removal, flash policy, or a megapixel floor for patient photos; treat those as house rules, not standards.

## Week 6 is the adherence cliff, and that is a different number from week 12

Two literatures give two cadences and the product needs both. The **efficacy horizon is 12 weeks**: the 2024 AAD acne guideline's evidence is anchored there, with topical retinoid versus vehicle IGA success at 12 weeks in four RCTs and benzoyl peroxide plus retinoid at 12 weeks in three ([JAAD](https://www.jaad.org/article/S0190-9622(23)03389-3/fulltext)). The **adherence horizon is weeks 2-6**: a pilot RCT using electronic monitoring measured adherence falling from **86% at week 1 to 36% at week 6**, another study found **48% at three months**, and patients deviated from directives **because of regimen complexity** ([Patient Prefer Adherence](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.2147/PPA.S117437)). A plan that only re-checks at twelve weeks checks in after most of the drop-off has already happened. The strongest single intervention in that review is also the most product-shaped: mean adherence over six weeks was **15 percentage points higher** in the group given a **physical demonstration of how to apply the medication** — evidence for per-step application demos the provider records once per product rather than per client, though note the studied intervention was an in-person demonstration and a recorded video is an extrapolation. The ACTUO cohort (**n = 643**, mild-to-moderate acne) found adherence to **adjuvant barrier-repair therapy** was independently a key factor in improvement ([PMC4567797](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4567797/)), so moisturizer and barrier steps belong in the plan as tracked steps, not optional extras.

Be careful about what this does and does not license. **No study was found that isolates document-design variables** — step count, visual schedule, written rationale, checkbox format — as the intervention; the simplicity evidence is about the *regimen*, not the *document*. No evidence was found either way on whether including a rationale ("why this product") helps. No evidence validates a 6-week or 8-week follow-up *visit* as a studied interval; the 6-to-8-week figures are onset-of-effect and purging-resolution timelines, and 12 weeks is a trial endpoint, not a studied follow-up policy. Purging timelines in circulation (begins week 2-3, peaks 3-4, eases by 6-8) trace to consumer and commercial clinical-content pages, not peer review, and the "improvement usually not seen for 6-8 weeks" line for tretinoin reaches this research as a **secondhand quotation of FDA labeling** ([Hello Wisp](https://hellowisp.com/blog/tretinoin-purge)) — and tretinoin is prescription-only and outside esthetician scope anyway. **No citable source was found for ramping schedules of OTC actives** (retinol, AHA/BHA, benzoyl peroxide, vitamin C); the searches returned scraped SEO content the notes explicitly flag as uncitable. So the plan builder should support a week-indexed ramp as a *structure the provider fills in*, and the app should not ship opinionated ramp defaults it cannot source.

On turnaround, real-world store-and-forward medians range from **31 minutes** in an urgent-care workflow ([HVPAA](https://hvpaa.org/implementation-and-evaluation-of-a-store-and-forward-teledermatology-workflow-in-an-urgent-care-center/)) to **21.4 hours** at a German university hospital ([Pumnea et al., JDDG](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ddg.70431)) to **2.4 ± 12.7 days** in a Spanish health area ([BMC Primary Care](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12875-024-02479-1)). The widely repeated 24-72 hour norm rests on weak sourcing. A solo provider cannot credibly promise hours, and an unmet promise is worse than a modest one — so if SlickChart surfaces an SLA to the client, 48-72 hours is the defensible number.

## No incumbent owns the structured async skincare consult

The landscape splits cleanly. Med-spa EMRs are strong where SlickChart will be compared against them — Aesthetic Record offers photos placed directly in the chart, **side-by-side comparisons against earlier visits**, customizable consent forms with e-signatures and treatment-package tracking ([Aesthetic Record](https://www.aestheticrecord.com/features/treat/photo-management/)) — but in that tier **"virtual consult" means a calendar appointment with a video link**: Zenoti's virtual appointments are literally a service type configured as virtual with a link sent by email or SMS ([Zenoti help](https://help.zenoti.com/en/articles/4065737-enable-virtual-appointments)), and Mangomint's nearest adjacent feature is a virtual waiting room. Nobody in that tier ships an async, photo-first, deliverable-producing consult. The only purpose-built competitors found, DermAbyss and Pomp, are small and new ([DermAbyss](https://dermabyss.com/); [Pomp](https://builtin.com/company/pomp)) — low competitive risk, and correspondingly no proven demand signal either. DermAbyss markets a "DPI progress tracking" metric with no published definition; treat it as marketing.

The best-documented plan model in any adjacent category is **Practice Better's protocol templates**, which "combine food, supplement, and lifestyle recommendations into comprehensive care plans that you can adapt for individual client needs" ([Practice Better help](https://help.practicebetter.io/hc/en-us/articles/115004180527-Working-with-Protocol-Templates)) — a reusable multi-section structured document with per-client override, not a letter — and which becomes a sales instrument: publishing it **auto-creates a matching product recommendation and emails the client a cart ready for checkout**, with layered clinic-wide and per-client discounts, and a toggle to **exclude an item or a whole protocol from syncing** when it is recommended for reference only ([Fullscript + Practice Better](https://practicebetter.io/integrations/fullscript)). That last control is the telling detail: providers want to recommend things they are not selling, and a tool that forces every plan line into a cart gets resisted. The dermatology-side analogue is a composable PDF report assembled from selected sections ([DermEngine](https://www.dermengine.com/blog/top-10-features-intelligent-dermatology-software)).

Two white spaces are worth naming. **No vendor documents plan versioning, revision history, or an explicit "re-issue at follow-up"** — these plans are edited in place. An immutable issued-plan history with a diff against the previous issue would be differentiating rather than catch-up. And **no vendor documents photo-submission reminders or scheduled post-plan check-ins**, despite a direct search; what is documented is intake-completion chasing, with Practice Better auto-sending forms on booking and **re-sending automatically when still incomplete 24 hours before the visit** ([Practice Better](https://practicebetter.io/blog/how-to-automate-client-engagement-for-better-efficiency-in-2026), vendor-authored) and Zanda shipping a named "Incomplete Forms Automation" ([Zanda](https://support.zandahealth.com/incomplete-forms-automation)). That mechanic ports directly to the dominant stall state in a photo-first consult — **intake complete, photos not sent** — and nobody appears to be automating it.

The recurring complaints across this category read as a specification in negative form: **price and add-on creep**, **setup complexity** (Practice Better rates **4.7/5 on G2 across roughly 284 reviews** yet is consistently described as "overwhelming at first" — [G2](https://www.g2.com/products/practice-better/reviews?qs=pros-and-cons); [ChoosingTherapy](https://www.choosingtherapy.com/practice-better-review/), affiliate review site), **calendar drift**, and above all **template rigidity**: Jane App is faulted for "limited flexibility in customizing chart templates" with charting setup "the biggest downside" for new users ([Software Advice](https://www.softwareadvice.com/compare/81975-simplepractice/vs/86099-Jane-App/)). Practice Better's own weak-spot list includes **"deeper progress tracking"** — exactly what a photo-based skincare consult is good at. Forcing a payment processor also generates real resentment; Jane Payments becoming mandatory in May 2026 was called a "cash grab" by some users ([Pabau](https://pabau.com/blog/jane-app-vs-simple-practice/), vendor-authored comparison).

On AI, the evidence supports a narrow role. The only published validation found for cosmetic AI skin analysis is Perfect Corp's — **69% overall agreement** with a board-certified dermatologist across 14 characteristics, highest for erythema (83.7%) and wrinkles (81.6%), test-retest reliability 95% — reported by a third party citing a named 2022 journal paper that was not located directly ([The ACare](https://www.theacare.de/post/how-accurate-is-ai-skin-analysis-what-skincare-brands-should-know)), and the same analysis notes there is **no universal benchmark and that cosmetic skin analysis tools are not regulated devices**. Competitors publish no validation at all. Sixty-nine percent agreement with one reader is adequate for a descriptive provider-facing aid and inadequate for anything a client could read as a finding — and note that the two best-performing attributes are the two most visually obvious. The practice-software side adds the behavioral lesson: built-in AI note-taking is criticized for notes needing substantial revision and for offering no control of style, tone or structure, and default-on AI transcription drew press criticism in 2026 ([ClearHealthCosts](https://clearhealthcosts.com/blog/2026/07/therapy-company-simplepractice-adds-ai-transcripts-by-default/)). **Draft for the provider, never for the client; never on by default; never the thing that is sent.**

## What to borrow from GoHighLevel: four mechanics, and two warnings

The clearest finding in the GoHighLevel research is what it does *not* have. **It has no consultation, photo-documentation or clinical-records feature.** The only image primitive is a generic form file-upload field ([Growthable](https://growthable.io/gohighlevel-tutorials/forms/how-to-use-file-upload-custom-field-in-forms-and-surveys-for-gohighlevel/)); there is no before/after comparison, no face mapping, no per-visit photo series and no treatment-note object. HIPAA is a paid add-on requiring a signed BAA rather than a default, and GoHighLevel's own resellers tell clinics to **keep clinical records, SOAP notes and treatment documentation in a separate system** and use GoHighLevel for marketing ([GHL Experts](https://www.ghlexperts.com/guides/gohighlevel-hipaa-compliance)), with med-spa advice explicitly recommending two systems ([Net Partners](https://netpartners.marketing/crm-for-med-spas/)). Its beauty-vertical usage is overwhelmingly front-of-funnel and between-visits: lead capture, reminders, win-backs, review requests, retail upsell ([Softomate](https://www.softomatesolutions.com/blog/gohighlevel-uk-beauty-salons-aesthetics-clinics-2026/) — reseller; its percentage performance claims are unsourced marketing and are not repeated here). **The consult is a hole in GoHighLevel**, and that is a clean positioning boundary rather than a competitive threat.

Worth borrowing, in order: the **custom-value / custom-field split** (covered above, and the single best idea in the product for this use case); **snippets as distinct from templates**; **one named sequence with several ways in** — GoHighLevel workflows can hold multiple triggers so a contact enters from a form submit, a status change or a manual tag ([Growthable](https://www.growthable.io/gohighlevel-tutorials/workflows/list-of-workflow-triggers-for-gohighlevel/)), and the lesson for a single-purpose app is that a "post-consult follow-up" should be enterable by finishing a consult, by a form returning, *or* by her tapping a button on the client record; and **a tracked link whose click is an event**, not just an analytics row ([SupplyGem](https://supplygem.com/gohighlevel-trigger-links/)), which is what makes "nudge again in three days unless she opened it" possible. Conditional branching belongs **in the intake form and nowhere else** — GoHighLevel's Conditional Logic v2 can show/hide fields, skip slides and disqualify based on answers ([help.gohighlevel.com](https://help.gohighlevel.com/support/solutions/articles/155000005564-conditional-logic-in-surveys), vendor page, blocked, title and summary only) — which is the difference between a 40-question form nobody finishes and a 12-question one shaped by the answers. Branching inside the follow-up sequence is where complexity metastasizes.

Reject the rest, and reject it for a reason: the visual workflow canvas spans **100+ actions across 14 categories** ([Growthable](https://growthable.io/gohighlevel-tutorials/workflows/list-of-workflow-actions-for-gohighlevel/)) and is the direct cause of the most consistent complaint in the product, a **60-to-90-day learning curve** ([The Stack Insiders](https://www.thestackinsiders.com/blog/gohighlevel-reddit) — affiliate site summarizing r/gohighlevel, the only route to that community available here), with Capterra reviewers reaching "DO EVERYTHING, but DO NOTHING WELL" ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/177156/HighLevel/reviews/), via search summary). A solo esthetician will never open a node graph. The right surface is **the output of a workflow builder — a short list of named, editable sequences with a wording field and a timing field** — not the builder. Pipelines, round-robin, collective booking, snapshots and the agency layer are all multi-operator or reseller artifacts.

Two warnings transfer whether or not any feature does. **Email deliverability is architectural**: GoHighLevel's shared Mailgun sending pool means one tenant's bad behavior damages everyone's reputation ([MailFlow Authority](https://mailflowauthority.com/gohighlevel-email/gohighlevel-deliverability-problems) — deliverability vendor, incentivized), and any multi-tenant app sending client-facing mail on providers' behalf inherits that; plan per-provider domain authentication before volume, not after the first spam-folder complaint. **And lock-in is a differentiator available for free**: GoHighLevel's snapshots import only into GoHighLevel, and its API "cannot read what is inside a workflow — it only exposes workflow names and status" ([Meticulosity](https://www.meticulosity.com/gohighlevel-to-hubspot-migration) — migration vendor, incentivized, but a specific checkable claim). "Your wording, your templates and your plans are yours and exportable" is a promise SlickChart can make and mean.

## Compliance: the wording is the exposure, and it is state law, not settled law

**None of this is legal advice, and the authoritative answer in every case is the provider's own state board and her own insurer.** The research could not open HHS/OCR, the FTC's own pages, ASCP, AmSpa, or any state board primary text beyond two URLs.

The consistent prohibitions across available sources are **no diagnosis of injury, illness or disease; no prescribing of oral or topical medication; no treatment penetrating below the epidermis** — but the clearest statements located are single-state and must not be generalized. Illinois is reported to prohibit diagnosing medical skin conditions, naming melanoma, rosacea, severe cystic acne and psoriasis as examples ([via AmSpa](https://americanmedspa.org/news/illinois-releases-statement-on-prohibited-practices-for-aestheticians) — domain blocked, issuing body and exact wording **unconfirmed**); California commentary states the scope is "limited to beautification, relaxation, and non-invasive services only" with multi-agency enforcement ([DJ Holt Law](https://djholtlaw.com/the-scope-of-practice-for-estheticians-in-california/)); Texas has considered legislation on the same line, so it is still being redrawn by statute ([AmSpa](https://americanmedspa.org/news/texas-bill-would-prohibit-esthetician-from-performing-medical-treatments)). **No state board guidance on virtual or remote esthetic services was found at all**, and that absence is itself the most important thing to tell Ashley: nobody has published the rule for what she is already doing.

Two findings drive product design. First, **North Carolina's board has issued a declaratory ruling on esthetic scope turning on the "intent of the service"** ([NC Board of Cosmetic Art, PDF](https://www.nccosmeticarts.com/uploads/forms/Esthetic%20Scope%20Intent%20of%20the%20Service%20Declaratory%20Ruling.pdf), not read in full) — meaning the *purpose* a service is described as serving, not only the technique, can pull it into medical scope. If that reasoning generalizes, **the wording of the plan carries regulatory risk independent of what the provider actually does**: "to treat your acne" and "to improve the look and feel of your skin" are not interchangeable. This rests on one state's ruling that could not be read in full and should be flagged as such, but it makes templated client-facing language a compliance surface, not merely a UX one. The implementable version is a vocabulary constraint: disease nouns are diagnostic claims, observation language is not, and the app can lint provider free-text for disease nouns and offer the observational rewrite. The 21% false-reassurance finding adds the complementary rule — **the app should be structurally incapable of saying a lesion looks fine.** Offer "documented" and "referred," never "benign."

Second, the privacy picture does not depend on whether Ashley is a HIPAA covered entity. A cash-pay solo esthetician with no electronic claims transactions probably is not ([Accountable HQ](https://www.accountablehq.com/post/hipaa-security-for-med-spas-compliance-checklist-and-best-practices) — compliance vendor, directional only), but **Washington's My Health My Data Act, effective 31 March 2024, was expressly contemplated as reaching cash-only and "health-adjacent" services**, and requires a published consumer-health-data policy, **affirmative consent before collecting or sharing**, consumer rights, reasonable security and written processor contracts ([Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2023/04/my-health-my-data-washington-enacts-first-state-comprehensive-health-privacy-law); [Crowell](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/washington-enacts-my-health-my-data-act-to-strengthen-protections-for-health-data)). Separately, the **FTC's amended Health Breach Notification Rule took effect 29 July 2024** and clarifies application to direct-to-consumer health apps **not regulated by HIPAA**, with requirements reaching third-party service providers ([McDermott](https://www.mwe.com/?p=279089); [Orrick](https://infobytes.orrick.com/2024-05-03/ftc-finalizes-new-rule-health-data-breach-notification-requirements/) — consistent across independent law-firm alerts, but secondary). The inference, not a legal conclusion: **a multi-tenant SaaS storing client skin photos and health-ish intake is plausibly itself within that rule's reach**, so SlickChart's own breach-notification posture is in scope, not only the provider's. Designing to MHMDA is the conservative single national target, and it has a direct schema consequence: **photo consent must be stored as structured per-purpose flags on the photo** (chart only / education / social / paid ads), defaulting to chart-only, individually withdrawable — not one boolean on the client. Prevailing practice agrees that service consent is not photo consent and that a client must be able to refuse photography and still receive the service ([Pure Spa Direct](https://purespadirect.com/blogs/pure-spa-direct-blog/hipaa-lite-thinking-for-photos-dms-and-booking-notes-your-spas-guide-to-smart-not-scary-client-privacy)).

One more item is worth raising with Ashley even though it could not be verified. The single most relevant insurance guidance found — **ASCP's own**, reached only through a search summary of a blocked page and originating in COVID-era FAQ content whose current status is unconfirmed — states that liability policies can cover virtual consultations including "developing treatment plans for future in-person appointments," conditioned on the **client being physically located in a state where the provider is licensed**, a full intake form for new clients, the provider being able to see the client, sessions being **live and not prerecorded**, and staying within scope ([ASCP](https://www.ascpskincare.com/node/2466)). If that is still ASCP's position, two of those conditions cut against an asynchronous photo consult, and the state condition is implementable as a guardrail: capture the client's state at consult time and warn when it differs from the provider's license state. **No esthetics interstate compact was found** — unlike nursing or medicine — so the conservative reading is that remote esthetic advice does not travel across state lines. No case law, claims data or enforcement example involving an esthetician and remote written advice was located, so how this risk has actually materialized is unknown. **This is the highest-value thing for Ashley to confirm with her own carrier, and it is a question for her, not for us to answer.**

## Prioritized recommendations

Ordered by value-to-effort. Sizes are rough and assume the existing consult-type library, intake form and review screen stay as they are.

| # | Recommendation | Size |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Structured plan document with required fields, delivered as a client-facing page at a stable link | Large (stage it) |
| 2 | Per-provider editable message wording with account-level constants and tokens | Small-medium |
| 3 | Reusable plan templates, holistic and corrective variants shipped editable | Medium |
| 4 | Follow-up date on every plan, plus week-2 and week-6 check-ins driven by consult status | Small-medium |
| 5 | Auto-compose the product-audit verdicts into the plan ("keep / change / stop") | Small |
| 6 | Scope-safe language lint and a referral block that cannot say "benign" | Small |
| 7 | "Photos not submitted" nudge and a stale-photo warning | Small |
| 8 | Intake: require prior treatments and outcome, current actives, pregnancy and isotretinoin gates | Medium |
| 9 | Per-purpose photo consent flags on the photo | Small-medium |
| 10 | Issued-plan history with a diff, and re-issue at follow-up | Medium |
| 11 | Baseline-ghosted photo retake at two proximities | Medium |
| 12 | Google Calendar busy-time import with visible connection health | Large |

**1. The structured plan document.** Replace the single textarea with a sectioned builder whose output renders as a client-facing page reachable from the client PWA's existing magic link, and reduce the chat message to a pointer plus one line. Required per-product fields: name, one-phrase purpose, amount as a physical referent, slot (AM / PM / weekly), position in order, named days, week-indexed ramp, and do-not-combine. The slot is the organizing key; the product is the child record. Section order as proposed above — goal, what to do tonight, PM, AM, weekly, phases, what to expect with dates, cautions and stop rules, see-a-doctor block, next check-in with photo instructions, product list last. Cap at five products with a warning above that. Compose the existing product plan and home-care list *into* this document rather than duplicating them. The evidence: the explicitness gradient (89% vs 53%), "use as directed" as a documented confusion source, omission as the default failure (42-45%), the Universal Medication Schedule's fixed slots, the AVS critique that unprioritized comprehensiveness goes unread, and the 3-5 product ceiling. **Stage it**: a sectioned document with required fields and a client page is most of the value; phases, per-step demo images and the checklist can follow. Note explicitly that the section list is our construction — no published standard exists.

**2. Editable wording.** As specified above. Account-level constants plus per-consult-type overrides plus a live preview, with snippets on the same storage.

**3. Plan templates.** Reusable, per-provider, fully editable, with a holistic variant that carries lifestyle and internal-support sections and a corrective variant that does not. Template rigidity is the single most consistent complaint about the incumbents, and the holistic/clinical divide changes sections, not adjectives. Ship both pre-written because solo operators do not build from empty.

**4. Follow-up cadence.** A required follow-up date on every issued plan, with automatic check-ins at roughly week 2 (tolerance and irritation) and week 6 (is it improving). This is the clearest evidence-anchored number in the research: the adherence cliff is week 6, not week 12. Keep the sequence linear, enterable from several places, and cancellable by the client opening the plan.

**5. Quote her own shelf back.** The review screen already captures per-product feedback on what she uses now; compose those into a keep / change / stop block in the plan automatically. Near-free, and it directly counters the dominant client complaint that the consult was a sales pitch.

**6. Language guardrails.** Lint provider free-text for disease nouns and outcome promises; offer the observational rewrite; make the referral block offer "documented" and "referred" with no field that can assert a lesion is benign; include a plan footer stating the recommendations are cosmetic and not medical advice. Driven by the intent-of-the-service doctrine and the 21% false-reassurance finding. No association model disclaimer wording could be retrieved, so have a professional review the footer text.

**7-9.** The photo nudge generalizes the one automation vendors actually document (incomplete-form re-send at T-24h) to the real stall state in a photo-first consult. Stale-photo warning follows from "condition evolves between capture and read." Intake additions follow from the 2016 secret-shopper finding that the failure was not asking — keep the medical-history items framed as **safety screening and referral triage only**, never as assessment. Per-purpose consent flags follow from MHMDA's affirmative-consent structure and are a schema decision that is cheap now and expensive later.

**10-11.** Plan versioning is a documented white space — no vendor describes it — and a re-issue flow with a diff is where the consult becomes a relationship rather than a one-shot deliverable. Ghosted baseline capture at two proximities is the home-achievable substitute for standardized lighting and matters specifically because lighting distorts apparent inflammation.

**12. Calendar.** As specified. Real, wanted, and last.

**Architecture constraints that apply to all of the above.** Every new object here accumulates, so each needs a merge decision before it ships, per CLAUDE.md §0.6-0.8. Issued plans are append-only history and union cleanly by id with the newest `_ts` winning; the *working draft* is a single editable value where newest wins; plan and message templates are authored content that can be deleted, so they need `_mergeAuthoredById` with their own tombstone key in `_TOMB_OBJ` rather than a plain union that would resurrect a template she removed. Do not put a key whose entries can legitimately be removed into the list-union set. The client-facing copy of a plan has to land where `_unionClientForms` will union it, alongside `c.summaries`. Every new loader needs its own `_reloadStep` and a place in `_reloadAll()`, or a plan written on her phone lands on her laptop's disk and never reaches the screen. And the client-facing plan page is reached by a magic-link token that must expose exactly one client's own record and nothing of any other provider's.

## What to reject, and why

**Client-facing AI skin scores or grades.** The only published validation found is 69% agreement with a single dermatologist, the category is explicitly unregulated, and the two attributes that performed best are the two most visually obvious. On-device measurements can stay where they are — provider-facing, descriptive, a suggestion she confirms. Nothing a client could read as a finding.

**AI that writes the message she sends.** The named complaints about built-in AI note-taking are that output needs substantial revision, that style, tone and structure cannot be adjusted, and — the reputational failure to avoid outright — that it was switched on by default. Draft for the provider, behind an explicit action, always editable, never auto-sent.

**A workflow canvas, pipelines, or anything multi-operator.** The canvas is the cause of the learning-curve complaint it is meant to solve. Ship the output of a workflow builder: a few named sequences, each with a wording field and a timing field.

**A platform product storefront or default affiliate links.** Affiliate rates on an emailed product list run meaningfully high and reorder subscriptions compound them ([StackedSkincare](https://stackedskincare.com/pages/affiliates); [IMAGE Skincare](https://imageskincare.com/pages/image-affiliate-program), snippet-sourced), so links must be the provider's own or the feature takes money off her. Copy Practice Better's "reference only" toggle, not its dispensary. Forcing a processor is separately resented.

**A fee-credit ledger.** Crediting part of the consult fee toward a later product order looks close to standard practice among paid consult offerings, commonly around half, across roughly seven live pages — but **every one of those figures is a search snippet from a page that could not be opened, no price or policy was verified, and no independent conversion data exists at all** (the only conversion figures found were vendor marketing, unsourced and internally inconsistent, and are excluded here). Build the cheap version — a per-provider credit-policy constant that renders as one plain line on the plan — and defer the two-step money flow until Ashley says she wants it.

**Live video, built in-house.** A commodity to integrate or to leave to the provider's own link; IntakeQ simply ships a Google Meet integration and Zenoti just stores and sends a link.

**Clinical-grade photo apparatus.** Color calibration, lesion measurement, DICOM/PACS and payer billing export are hospital-tier. Enforced five-view capture is also wrong here: the consult-type library already names its own labels, so add a second proximity and the ghost overlay instead of imposing a clinical view set on every type. Lesion counting is unrealistic at scale and unreliable in non-specialist hands; if a severity scale is ever added, **IGA 0-4** is the right primitive — five grades, each with a written morphologic description designed to reduce interobserver variability ([Ann Dermatol 2023](https://anndermatol.org/DOIx.php?id=10.5021%2Fad.23.094)) — presented as an "appearance grade," never a diagnosis, and stored as (grade, region, date, photo-set) because IGA is static and change is a delta. Note that **no study was found on non-physician interrater reliability for IGA**, which is a real unknown.

**Opinionated ramp defaults and purging timelines in shipped copy.** No citable source exists for OTC active ramping schedules, and the purging timelines in circulation trace to commercial content pages. Give her the structure; let her fill in the numbers she stands behind.

**Spintax, snapshots, courses, memberships, funnels.** Different products wearing the same login, and spintax exists to evade carrier filtering on bulk cold sends — a practice a provider messaging her own clients should not be nudged toward.

## Conclusion

The most useful reframe in this research is that the consult's deliverable is not documentation — it is the product, and four independent lines of evidence land on the same object. It is what the client was promised and what she judges the service by; it is the after-hours unpaid labor a software feature can actually displace; it is where the provider's product revenue sits; and it is where scope exposure lives, because at least one state board's reasoning turns on the *stated purpose* of a service rather than only the technique. A free-text box in a chat thread fails all four of those jobs at once, which is why "the follow-up documents need more structure" is not a polish request. The structure to give it, though, is nobody's published standard — it has to be derived from comprehension evidence and labeled honestly as derived, because the alternative is inventing a standard and then citing ourselves.

The second reframe is about where the research is thin, and it is thin in a patterned way. Everything about *mechanism* — comprehension, adherence decay, concordance, the technical reason one-way calendar sync drifts — is reasonably well evidenced even through a blocked proxy. Everything about *this specific user* is missing: no practitioner said any of this in her own words, no US client account of a solo esthetician's virtual consult was reachable, no conversion data exists, no consumer-appetite survey later than 2021 was found, and no state board has published anything about remote esthetic advice at all. That asymmetry should set the order of work. The recommendations grounded in mechanism — the document, the explicitness rules, the week-6 cadence, the editable wording — are safe to build now. The ones that depend on knowing what providers want and what clients will pay for are better answered by shipping the cheap version to Ashley and watching what she actually does with it than by any further reading.
