# Rebuild the client file around the visit

The measurements of SlickChart's current client file describe a screen that spends its best attention on the least-used content and then hides the most-used content below the fold: **2201px of total length on a 390x844 phone for a client with three visits, of which the first 496px carries no clinical information at all**, the client's name renders twice, and the first two controls on the page are "Export file" and a red "Remove." Set that against the only measured evidence about long screens — NN/g's eyetracking study found **57% of viewing time above the fold and 81% within the first three screenfuls** (n=120, 130,000+ fixations, desktop) ([NN/g, Scrolling and Attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)) — and the cost is concrete: **Session summaries, the second-smallest section at 109px, sits 1414px down**, while Client details burns 393px on fields that are frequently empty. The fix is not more polish. Twelve competing products have converged on one answer the current file does not use: **a single reverse-chronological visit feed as the default view, with identity and alerts in a persistent frame and everything else collapsed behind a header that states what is inside it** (SimplePractice does it with four tabs where Boulevard has ten and Vagaro ~thirteen). Three things follow from that for SlickChart specifically. The virtual consult stops being a 286px text block at the bottom and becomes a *kind of visit* in the feed, with states, which is also the only way it stops feeling bolted on. Photos become one library with three indexes and a camera launched from the visit, because every documented cause of photo-workflow abandonment is a filing problem rather than a capture problem. And the structural bug — the provider's chart reading `sessionSummaries[id]` while the client's app reads `c.summaries` — gets fixed before any visual work, because a provider who cannot tell what her client sees cannot trust the screen, and Diana has already reported the symptom.

## The first 496 pixels hold no clinical content, and 15 of 22 controls are too small to hit

Three independent problems overlap in that opening half-screen, and all three have cheap fixes. The **duplicated name** (title bar plus a centred repeat) is pure waste in the region where attention is highest: NN/g's page-relative figures put **over 42% of viewing time in the top 20% of a page, and over 65% of first-screen viewing time in the top half of the viewport** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). The **"Export file" and red "Remove" pair in prime position** inverts frequency: these are rare, mostly irreversible administrative actions, and no competitor puts them first. Aesthetic Record's equivalent region is spent on **date of birth, age and allergies as position 1 of the panel, with commercial information demoted to position 5 and emergency contact at the very bottom** ([Aesthetic Record: patient side panel](https://learn.aestheticrecord.com/en/articles/10258025-viewing-and-managing-patient-side-panel-information-on-the-web)). Move export and remove into an overflow at the end of the file, where Boulevard-style destructive actions normally live, and keep the top for the three facts that change what the provider does in the next sixty seconds.

What belongs there is well modelled. Jane App is the only product publishing a true pinned summary bar, and its design lesson is that **every metric is also a navigation shortcut** — Total Bookings jumps to Appointments, Private Balance to Billing > Purchases — and that the bar **degrades by permission and plan**, hiding four of eight metrics from staff without billing access ([Jane: Patient Profile Dashboard](https://jane.app/guide/patient-profile-dashboard)). A solo esthetician needs a much shorter bar: visit count, time since last visit, next appointment, and alert chips. For the alerts, copy the cheapest documented pattern rather than a banner: Aesthetic Record renders **status icons directly on the patient's name — red for allergies, star for membership, a red timer for documents expiring or expired — and the same icons carry into its provider app** ([Aesthetic Record](https://learn.aestheticrecord.com/en/articles/10258025-viewing-and-managing-patient-side-panel-information-on-the-web)). Icon-only is the one thing to resist: NN/g's icon work is unambiguous that icons without labels can be perfectly legible and still functionally invisible — in one study a clock icon opening a navigation-history drawer was recognised as a clock and **zero participants clicked it** ([NN/g, Icon Usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/)). Chips get words.

The touch-target finding is the clearest correctness defect in the measurements. **15 of 22 visible interactive controls are under 40px tall.** WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.8 sets a Level AA floor of **24 x 24 CSS pixels** and explicitly names people with tremors, one-handed users and *people on moving transport* as beneficiaries — the closest the standard comes to the hurried, distracted, between-appointments case ([W3C, Understanding SC 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html)). But 24px is a conformance minimum, not a usability target. The older-adult experimental literature, modest and ageing as it is, found a **steep drop in target-touching errors between about 5mm and 8mm with smaller gains beyond**, and errors that skew toward *missing* the target rather than slipping off it ([Leitão & Silva, 40 older adults, targets 2–11mm](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/2821679.2831275); [Jin, Plocher & Kiff](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225367546_Touch_Screen_User_Interfaces_for_Older_Adults_Button_Size_and_Spacing)). Eight millimetres is roughly 44–48 CSS px at typical mobile densities, which is where Apple's 44pt and Google's 48dp conventions already sit. Design to 44–48px and treat 24px as the absolute floor; one study even reported a **2.3cm active area needed for 80% accuracy in the left quarter of the screen**, so a single fixed size will not serve every screen region equally ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/400160495_Optimization_of_Touch_Active_Area_Size_Based_on_Click_Position_Bias_in_Older_Adults'_Touchscreen_Interaction)).

Two layout habits visible in the measurements should be retired outright. **Empty fields rendering as full-height rows** ("Current routine / products —", "No date of birth") is how a 393px Client details section gets built out of mostly nothing; and **help text sitting in the data value column** ("Photo consent: Captured once they sign an intake") teaches the provider that the value column is unreliable. NN/g's own caution is worth hearing in the other direction too: designs have become **sparser than they need to be**, and "clean" on a dense reference screen is produced by typographic hierarchy and honest grouping, not by padding ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)).

## One visit feed can replace eight to eleven sections

The single most consequential measurement is not a pixel count: **the section count varies between 8 and 11 depending on what data the client has, so the screen's shape changes per client.** For an infrequent, non-technical user that is the worst property a reference screen can have, because the only reliable way to find anything is spatial memory. Baymard's partially-matching-category finding sharpens it — users open the first label that plausibly fits and stop ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/main-navigation-product-categories)) — which means the current file's overlapping "Private notes," "Session summaries," "Recommended routine" and "Recommended products" headings will produce real misses regardless of styling.

The category's answer is a merged chronological stream plus a persistent frame, and the clinical products have gone furthest. SimplePractice runs the whole client record on **four tabs — Overview, Billing, Measures, Files — with Overview as the default and "a chronological view of client activity" covering appointments and documents together**, and it buys that compression by turning document type into a *filter* (an "All items" dropdown holding chart notes, intake questionnaires and scored measures) and verbosity into a *view mode*: **List (no note content), Excerpt (short previews), Expanded (full note text)**, with a date-range filter over the top ([SimplePractice support](https://support.simplepractice.com/hc/en-us/articles/48323197009677)). Those three densities are the most underrated pattern in this research: they answer "scan ten visits" and "read one visit" on the same screen with no drill-down, and for SlickChart they replace both the separate summaries section and most of the reason to scroll. Mangomint's Timeline does the same job for a salon product, tracking **upcoming and past appointments, completed sales, timeline notes and form submissions with a filter to show one type** ([Mangomint: Client Timeline](https://www.mangomint.com/learn/client-timeline/)).

Two devices keep a feed from burying what must always be read. The first is pinning with a hard cap: Mangomint allows **up to three pinned timeline notes and up to three pinned form submissions** via a Pin to Top action, and pinning *moves* the entry rather than duplicating it ([Mangomint](https://www.mangomint.com/learn/client-timeline/)); Jane goes further with two levels, where **starring holds a whole chart entry at the top of the list and pinning renders items as cards across the top of the chart** ([Jane: Pinning and starring your chart entries](https://jane.app/guide/pinning-and-starring-your-chart-entries)); GlossGenius pins **key intake questions to the client profile "so they're easy to find before an appointment"** ([GlossGenius: forms & waivers](https://glossgenius.com/forms-waivers)). The cap is the copyable detail — it forces curation instead of letting the pinned area become a second feed. The second device is a routing rule rather than a layout, and it is the most transferable IA decision in this whole set. Fresha tells staff to choose between three tiers: a **staff alert** for "information that staff must see immediately, such as health concerns," a **client profile note** that is "available for every future appointment," and an **appointment note** for "a one-time update that only applies to a single visit" ([Fresha](https://www.fresha.com/help-center/knowledge-base/clients/101248-add-notes-to-client-profiles)). Phorest implements the middle two independently and surfaces all three horizons inside one appointment dialog — service notes, general client notes, and previous appointments' service notes ([Phorest](https://phorest.helpjuice.com/44077-clients-GB/333590-how-to-add-client-notes-on-your-system)). SlickChart's "Private notes" box is currently doing all three jobs at once, which is why it has to sit high and stay long.

For the sections that survive as libraries rather than as feed entries — forms and documents, recommended routine, recommended products, client details — collapse them, and put a **summary in the header rather than a label**. This is the single highest-confidence structural recommendation available, and it comes from measured behaviour rather than opinion: Baymard found participants rely on summaries to check their entries, and that accordions showing only step headers force people to reopen completed steps, with the remedy being to **collapse into summaries** ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/accordion-checkout-usability)). "Forms & documents" tells a provider nothing; "Forms & documents — 4, intake signed 14 Sep" lets her skip it. Collapsing is also how the file's useful content gets back into the first two screenfuls, which is where attention actually is, so the long-scroll and accordion positions are less opposed than they look. Be honest about the limits of this evidence: **no head-to-head study compares tabs, accordions, one long scroll and hub-and-spoke on a phone**, no mobile eyetracking percentages exist (the 57/74/81 figures are desktop at 1920x1080), and NN/g's accordion articles — which pre-date its own 2018 scroll data — lead a reader to the opposite conclusion from the scroll study ([NN/g, Accordions for Complex Content](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/accordions-complex-content/)). The reasons to prefer accordions over tabs here are narrower and defensible: tabs hide the *existence* of content, accordions hide only the content while keeping the inventory visible; and novice menu selection is visual search, roughly linear in item count, so **the lever is grouping and label quality rather than an arbitrary cap** ([HCI Book, Menu breadth and Miller's 7±2](https://www.hcibook.com/e3/online/menu-breadth/)). The much-quoted "5 to 7 sections" rule is a misapplication of Miller and should not drive the design. Two mechanical cautions: NN/g reports mobile accordions can make users think a new page loaded, so **Back should collapse rather than leave** ([NN/g, Accordions on Mobile](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-accordions/)), and a sticky identity header has no supporting evidence at all — the summary-in-header pattern delivers "keep context visible" without spending permanent viewport, and a sticky bar that covers a focused element or an anchor target is a genuine accessibility defect.

What the feed must carry is set by esthetics rather than by software. Practitioner education is specific and dated: chart **the day of the treatment**, because "clients see their skin up close everyday and may not notice changes that you see," keep a **dedicated section for product purchases** because "staff may not check purchase history in other systems," ask new medical history **before every treatment** since clients return every **4 to 6 weeks**, and treat photos as non-optional — "Pictures are a must!" ([Wendy Polo, Charting as an Esthetician, Aesthetic Back Bar, 18 March 2024](https://aestheticbackbar.com/spa-management/charting-as-an-esthetician)). That source also supplies the strongest justification found anywhere for a one-screen file: the pace of the work means other systems do not get checked, so key information has to live **inside the chart itself**. The realistic budget is tight — a practitioner guide describes estheticians building **five to ten minutes of note-writing time between appointments** (educational site, no methodology; treat as heuristic) ([Luminous Skin Lab](https://www.luminousskinlab.com/esthetician-education/new-esthetician-getting-started-guide/client-confidence-consultations/how-to-document-client-notes-properly.php)) — and phone-first charting is genuinely underserved rather than merely uneven: ClinicSense's own documentation recommends **the desktop platform for SOAP notes and intake forms** ([ClinicSense](https://clinicsense.com/professions/massage-therapy-software)), and reviewers describe waiting to enter notes at home. The esthetician record also differs from both neighbouring trades in a way that should shape the feed: a stylist's formula card is a lookup and a massage SOAP note is a per-session record, but a skin chart is **a longitudinal series about one body surface on a 4–6 week cadence, where the comparison between visits is the clinical content**. That argues for a "last visit" block above the feed rather than leaving the provider to derive it: SimplePractice ships exactly that as **Session Prep**, a pre-appointment summary combining the most recent progress note, treatment plans and chart details ([SimplePractice Care Aide](https://www.simplepractice.com/lp/care-aide/)), and Boulevard ships the write side as **Copy Previous Chart, which duplicates the last completed chart for the same template but deliberately leaves the signature and date blank** ([Boulevard](https://support.boulevard.io/en/articles/11157720-forms-and-charts-copy-previous-chart)) — the most copyable single interaction in this research, because it gives speed without letting an attestation ride along.

## Two stores hold the same summary, and the chart shows the wrong one

This is the first thing to fix, ahead of all layout work, and the code confirms the measurement. The chart's summaries row reads `sessionSummaries[id]` and renders "`N` saved summar(y/ies)" with the empty text "Finish a session to save one here" (slickchart.html ~line 8353), while the client PWA reads `summaries` off the provider's client record. Saving a visit currently writes both (`sessionSummaries[id].unshift(_rec)` and `c.summaries.unshift(entry)`, ~lines 11859–11874), but nothing reconciles them afterwards, and three paths can pull them apart: edits and deletes re-find the client-side copy by **heuristic match** — `ts`, else `date` plus `title` (~lines 12065–12110) — and silently skip when neither matches; the two stores merge through **different functions with different dedup keys** (`sc_session_summaries` through `_CLIENT_MAP_LIST`'s entry union on `_draftDate||ts||date`, `c.summaries` through `_unionClientForms` on `_draftDate||ts||date|title`); and any record created or imported with only one side populated — exactly the seeded client that displayed "0 saved summaries" against three summaries in `c.summaries` — reads as empty. That is a provider being told her client's visit history does not exist while the client is looking at it, which is the precise shape of Diana's repeated report that a client's summary disappears while other content survives.

Two changes close it. **Make the chart read the client-facing copy** and derive the provider view from it, so there is one store of record for the thing the client sees; keep `sc_session_summaries` only if it holds provider-only fields the client must not see, in which case the chart's counter still comes from `c.summaries` and the two are joined by a stable id rather than by date-and-title. Then **put "what your client sees" on the screen as a view, not an inference** — one tap from the summaries row. That is a direct answer to a complaint this research found at both ends of the market: the forms-to-chart disconnect appears in Mangomint reviews ("intake forms also do not automatically map into client account fields") and in GlossGenius reviews alike, so it is category-wide rather than a budget-tier compromise ([G2, Mangomint](https://www.g2.com/products/mangomint/reviews); [Capterra, GlossGenius](https://www.capterra.com/p/174830/GlossGenius/reviews/) — both via search summaries, not verbatim-verified fetches).

While the stores are being unified, fix the empty states, because on this codebase they are a correctness feature rather than decoration. NN/g's framing is that an empty state's first job is **status disambiguation** — is there no data, an error, or a pending load — and its worked example is a panel whose users re-ran the same query repeatedly because they could not tell which ([NN/g, Designing Empty States](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/empty-state-interface-design/)). Given a product history of silent sync failures, every library row needs three distinguishable states: *nothing saved yet*, *not loaded yet*, and *this did not load* with the reason. One caution on the adjacent advice: Carbon recommends **starter content** for complex setups ([Carbon Design System](https://carbondesignsystem.com/patterns/empty-states-pattern/)), and in this codebase that collides directly with the boot-defaults-over-real-data problem — starter content must render as a prompt, never be seeded as records. Lastly, the divergence itself should be measurable: add a count of records present in one store and not the other to the founder recovery paste, in the same change, per the project's own intake rule.

## The virtual consult should be a visit, not the last section

The consult block's problems are positional and rhetorical, not functional. It is **286px at the bottom of a 2201px file**, which by the attention data is the worst real estate on the page, and **its most prominent line is a limitation** ("Provider-initiated, clients can't start one on their own"). Nothing about it reads as part of the chart. The fix that makes it native is structural: a consult is a *kind of visit*, so it belongs in the same reverse-chronological feed as every in-person visit, carrying its own explicit states — invited, submitted, in review, answered — exactly as Boulevard shows per-appointment forms with **"Not started" / "In progress"** statuses and draft saving ([Boulevard](https://support.boulevard.io/en/articles/8038055-forms-and-charts-in-the-professional-app)). Replace the limitation sentence with the action it implies: an "Invite her to send photos" button on the client file, which is what the sentence was describing anyway. Jane's tablet flow shows how little else is needed to make asynchronous work feel finished — after signing a chart, **a lock icon appears beside that appointment in Day view**, so the day's completion state is legible at a glance ([Jane](https://jane.app/guide/charting-on-a-mobile-device-tablet-or-smartphone)).

The provider's review surface should be one screen holding the intake answers next to the photos with a draft write-up already in an editable box, and the evidence for where the time goes is unusually clean. The diagnostic read is about **one minute per case** in pooled store-and-forward data, while in a lab study of 64 teledermatology consultations **filling out the referral form was the single most time-consuming task at 3:12 of an 11:32 mean consultation, 28% of the total** ([Int J Med Inform](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1386505607000755); [JMIR Dermatology review, 2022](https://derma.jmir.org/2022/3/e37517)). Writing is the expense. Drafting is also the intervention with the most careful evidence, and it cuts both ways: Stanford's five-week study of **162 clinicians found reduced burden and burnout derivatives with 20% draft utilisation and no change in time spent** ([JAMA Netw Open 2024;7(3):e243201](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2816494)), a primary-care review found the reviewing physician judged **4 of 18 (22%) AI-drafted portal messages erroneous** (tiny sample, a warning rather than a rate) ([npj Digital Medicine 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-01586-2)), and patients accept drafts **only conditional on a clinician reviewing and approving before sending** ([JAMA Netw Open 2026](https://www.ovid.com/journals/janop/fulltext/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.22463~patient-perspectives-on-ai-drafted-electronic-portal)). Draft for the provider, behind an explicit action, always editable, never auto-sent — which is where the earlier rounds of this project already landed. Two details belong on that screen for clinical reasons established earlier in this project and reinforced here: **show the photos' age prominently** (lesions had changed in 87% of patients by the time of an in-person visit in one series) and keep history fields **required**, because requests for patient history *fell* as image quality rose — 64% unassisted, 59% assisted, 47% resident-taken — so good-looking photos actively suppress asking ([PMC12488292](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12488292/)). Expect roughly half of consults to end in "come in" rather than "here's your answer": in a single-practice patient-initiated series, **60 of 112 photos from 85 patients (53.6%) were resolved electronically** ([PMC10452479](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10452479/)), so "book this" is a first-class outcome, not an exception path.

**Do not rebuild the client-side capture flow.** The measurements describe six illustrated tips, per-photo contextual hints that vary by label, and a pre-send self-check with tap-to-retake, and that is already at or beyond the documented state of the art. A peer-reviewed inventory of **191 skin-imaging apps found only 57% had even one of 14 acquisition features and 66% had one or none**, with imaging tips and multiple-image requirements the two most common, and **no grid feature and no voice guidance anywhere in the 14** ([Sun et al., *Skin Research and Technology* 2022, PMC9907654](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9907654/)). Curology, the best-documented direct-to-consumer flow, publishes a rule set of **three photos — front and each profile — face filling the frame, natural light, no makeup, no filters, no mirror selfies** and ships **no good-versus-bad examples at all** ([Curology: Uploading photos](https://support.curology.com/en_us/uploading-photos-B1pwm2d2d)). Note plainly that the same 191-app paper **counted features and did not test efficacy**, so any claim that tips or overlays improve image quality is currently unevidenced. What is worth *adding* is the one attribute with a measured effect: in a survey of 10 dermatologists reviewing **2,915 patient-submitted images, in-focus images had 5.56 times the odds (95% CI 4.63–6.67) of being considered useful for medical decision-making**, while **55.1% were useful, 62.2% were of sufficient quality and 8.9% did not depict a skin condition at all** ([JAMA Dermatology, via ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362298942_Quality_and_Perceived_Usefulness_of_Patient-Submitted_Store-and-Forward_Teledermatology_Images)). So: automatic blur/focus detection with auto-reject-and-retry at capture, and a dumb is-there-skin-in-frame check at submit. Everything else in the flow is already better than the market.

On the client-facing side more broadly, the portal literature argues against spending effort where it is tempting to spend it. In a nationally representative US sample of **4,815 adults, 58.7% had not accessed an online medical record in a year**, and the top barriers were **preferring in-person communication (64.0%) and seeing no need (49.0%)**, with **"difficulty logging on" last at 19.0%** ([JMIR 2020;22(9):e18870](https://www.jmir.org/2020/9/e18870/)). Login polish will not move adoption; what moves it is something specific waiting for you. A preregistered RCT of **5,009 patients** found a one-time email after lab results became available tripled one-week enrolment (**10.8% vs 3.9%, OR 2.99**) but left long-term use looking like any other user's (**36.5% vs 33.1% at one year**) ([JAMIA Open 2026;9(3):ooag064](https://academic.oup.com/jamiaopen/article/9/3/ooag064)), while an observational complement found note-viewing **collapsed when email alerts ceased (RR 0.20, 95% CI 0.17–0.23)** ([systematic review, PMC6696508](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6696508/)). The analogue is exact: notify the client when something of *hers* changes — a new summary, new photos, an updated plan — and never on a schedule. And a VA prospective cohort of **270 patients tracked 18 months** found early behaviour strongly predictive (of 69 patients with 0–1 logins at six months, **46 (66.7%) stayed in the lowest band**) with **visits to the portal before identity verification** among the significant predictors of sustained use ([JMIR 2017;19(10):e345](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5663951/)) — which is an argument for the magic-link model SlickChart already has, and for the client seeing real content on first arrival rather than a sign-up wall. Mangomint markets account-free form access on exactly that logic ([Mangomint: Integrated forms](https://www.mangomint.com/features/integrated-forms/)). One honesty note: there is **no rigorous or peer-reviewed study comparing magic links with passwords or OTP**; the circulating 40–60% sign-up lifts and 20–30% abandonment drops are vendor claims and should not be cited.

## Photos need one library, two artifacts, and a ghost

The false choice in the brief's framing — per visit, per body area, or one gallery — is resolved the same way in every shipped product: **the store is one library and visit, area, procedure and tag are indexes over it**, exposed as saved views. Pabau's Photos tab carries a **three-way toggle of Timeline, Grid and Albums**, groups comparison-sidebar photos by date, and offers an album dropdown that filters to all photos, one album, or **uncategorised** ([Pabau](https://support.pabau.com/en/pabau2/how-to-compare-before-and-after-photos-in-the-client-card-0)); its documented behaviour that **deleting an album leaves photos in the gallery as uncategorised** confirms the gallery is canonical, not the album ([Pabau](https://support.pabau.com/en/pabau2/how-to-create-an-album-in-the-client-card-0)). Model a photo as `{clientId, visitId, area, tag[], ts}` and build the views as filters. The existence of that "uncategorised" filter is also the warning: **area and album tagging gets skipped at capture**, so any such field must be optional and backfillable or it becomes the reason capture does not happen.

Where the camera launches from is the whole game. Every documented cause of photo-workflow abandonment except one is a filing problem: inconsistent capture undermining trust in the comparisons, images **scattered across phones and personal devices**, the **"rename-and-upload ritual"**, and photos stored apart from the chart feeling like extra work (all vendor marketing, from [Consentz](https://www.consentz.com/plastic-surgery-photo-software-guide-for-clinics/), [RxPhoto](https://rxphoto.com/) and [PatientNow](https://getdemo.patientnow.com/use-cases/medical-aesthetic-clinics-and-med-spas); **no independent study of clinical-photography abandonment in aesthetics was found**, and the only non-vendor datapoint is an ophthalmology preprint identifying photography as a physical bottleneck). Aesthetic Record takes photos **directly from the chart, attaches them automatically to the visit, timestamps them and tags them to the procedure**; Zenoti launches the camera from **an appointment, a form, or a patient profile** with auto-linking to the chart ([Aesthetic Record](https://www.aestheticrecord.com/?p=50621); [Zenoti AI Photo Manager](https://www.zenoti.com/lp/ai-photo-manager) — both vendor marketing). Three entry points are what make photos land on the right visit without the provider thinking about filing.

For consistency, the cheapest complete implementation is already named by two independent products: **gridlines on the before photo, ghosting on the after photo**. TouchMD documents the ghost workflow — Overlays → Ghosting → pick the earlier photo → line the client up — with **opacity changed by dragging up or down anywhere on the image**, plus the instruction to use the earlier photo as a reference for camera distance and to match expression and pose ([TouchMD](https://help.touchmd.com/topics/using-touchmd-snap-pro-ghosting/)). RxPhoto implements the same thing with **a transparency lever on the left edge** and documents **ghosting several images consecutively**, which is the detail that matters: the real unit of capture is a *series*, and the ghost should auto-advance to the matching prior shot so a three-shot set costs barely more than one photo ([RxPhoto KB](https://kb.rxphoto.com/portal/en/kb/articles/photo-ghosting-on-the-rxphoto-mobile-app-iphone)). A drag-anywhere gesture beats an edge lever on a phone because it needs no target acquisition. The non-overlay half of the protocol is worth copying as copy rather than as code: Nextmotion's six-step session routine makes **"review the previous series to reproduce its setup and views"** step 3, *before* capture, and requires annotations and comparison panels to be made **on a labelled copy with originals retrievable** ([Nextmotion](https://www.nextmotion.net/blog/standardize-before-after-photos-aesthetic-clinic)). Step 3 is the strongest argument for putting last visit's photos inside the capture screen rather than in a gallery tab. Be clear about the evidence grade: **no peer-reviewed study shows ghost overlays improve reproducibility**, and vendor usage figures such as Zenoti's "2.5M+ photos captured, 1M+ using face guidelines," its claimed 2–3 minutes saved per appointment, and its "20–30% higher treatment acceptance" when before/afters are used in consults are all **unaudited marketing**.

Comparison on a phone has one strong layout and one weak one. **2-up side-by-side in portrait gives each pane about 45% of the width; a vertical-split slider on a single full-bleed image uses the whole screen and needs one thumb on a large target** — and Pabau's own gating is consistent with that reading, keeping slider and 2-up as the free core while putting overlay, stacked, 3/4/6-up and progress galleries behind a Care Plus subscription ([Pabau](https://support.pabau.com/en/pabau2/how-to-compare-before-and-after-photos-in-the-client-card-0)). Build the phone-safe selection route — **tick a checkbox on two photos, then Compare** — rather than drag-into-pane, which is a mouse gesture. Two cheap wins follow: make the saved comparison **a first-class object pinned to the top of the gallery** (Pabau's "Saved comparisons"), because the provider makes it once and then has it for the next consult and for sharing; and **default the two panes** rather than asking her to pick both, since Aesthetic Record's named presets are exactly "first visit vs most recent" and "before/after from the same treatment." Nobody documents synchronised pinch-zoom across panes, or what a slider does when two photos differ in aspect ratio, so both are open design problems rather than solved ones.

Storage and sharing follow from one architectural decision: **two artifacts per photo**. Clinicminds states it plainly — photos are **compressed when viewed or compared, while uploads keep their original resolution and file size, including on download or when sent to a patient** ([Clinicminds](https://help.clinicminds.com/en/articles/6502962-how-are-before-and-after-photos-displayed-in-clinicminds)) — and the reason it is safe is that a phone screen cannot render the original's detail anyway. No vendor reached publishes a per-patient cap; the only concrete volume figure found is a vendor estimate of **~200MB for a single photo-heavy patient** in an ~8-year-old help article ([SeriousMD](https://help.seriousmd.com/en/articles/1466539-how-big-of-a-storage-do-the-files-occupy-let-us-say-for-100-patients)), which sets the order of magnitude: the market's answer to volume is keep everything and serve a thumbnail. For consent, the documented failure mode is precise — **"even when consent is collected, it's not linked to specific photos, making it difficult for staff to verify what's approved"** ([RxPhoto](https://rxphoto.com/platform/forms-and-notes/patient-photo-release-form), vendor) — and the fix is per-use grants (clinical record / marketing / social) attached to the images they cover, enforced **at the moment the photo leaves the record**: Zenoti runs **a consent check before downloads** ([Zenoti](https://www.zenoti.com/lp/ai-photo-manager), vendor), and an NHS clinical photography service **photographs the signed consent form and uploads it to the record alongside the photos** ([West Herts Hospitals NHS](https://www.westhertshospitals.nhs.uk/patients-and-visitors/patient-information-leaflets/clinical-photography)) — the minimum viable version of the same idea. Vendor compliance claims are not legal guidance and were not independently verified. Because no product documents a per-photo "share with client" toggle, the obvious design is open: **default the client's view to nothing and let the provider mark a photo or a saved comparison client-visible**, which is also the natural unit to send. Finally, the project's own standing rule applies with force here: a recovery path nothing can reach is a bug, and `_openPhotoRecovery` is exactly the shape of thing that must be wired to a button in the new file or deleted.

## What to ship first, and what to leave alone

| Stage | Work | Why now |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Correctness** | Unify the summary stores on `c.summaries`, add a "what your client sees" view, make every library row distinguish *empty / not loaded / failed*, add store-divergence counts to the recovery paste | A provider cannot trust a screen that disagrees with her client's app; Diana's report is already open, and the empty-vs-failed distinction is a correctness issue on a codebase with silent-sync history |
| **2. The top of the file** | Name once; identity strip with visits, last visit, next appointment and labelled alert chips; "Export file" and "Remove" to an overflow at the end; every control to 44–48px; empty fields and help text out of the value column | Pure subtraction, no new data model, and it recovers most of the 496px where attention is highest |
| **3. The feed** | One reverse-chronological visit feed with List / Excerpt / Expanded densities and a type filter; a "last visit" block above it; pinning capped at three; collapse remaining libraries with counts and dates in their headers; route notes into alert / standing / this-visit | Turns a per-client 8-to-11-section screen into a fixed shape; this is the change that makes the file feel like one screen rather than a stack |
| **4. Consult and photos** | Consult as a feed entry with invited/submitted/in-review/answered states and an "invite her to send photos" action; review screen with intake beside photos, photo age shown, editable draft; camera launched from the visit; grid-before/ghost-after with the prior series on the capture screen; checkbox-then-Compare with a full-bleed slider; saved comparisons pinned; blur/focus auto-reject at capture | Each depends on the feed existing to land in; the capture additions are small and target the only attribute with a measured effect |
| **Later / deferred** | Client-visible photo marking and per-use consent enforcement at export; product recommendation in-place so `nav('shop')` can be client-scoped; event-triggered client notifications | Real, wanted, and none of it blocks the screen's shape |

Three things to leave alone. **The client-side capture flow** — it already exceeds the documented market and the published evidence cannot tell you which additions help. **A sticky identity header** — the evidence for it is nonexistent, the accessibility risk is real, and summaries-in-headers deliver the same orientation for free. And **a tab strip**: it is the category's dominant pattern precisely because the category's products carry commerce surfaces a solo esthetician does not have (Boulevard spends tabs on Wallet, Memberships, Packages, Products and Loyalty; a solo file can drop all of them), and tabs hide the existence of content from exactly the user who does not know what the screen contains. The one piece of the brief that remains genuinely unresolved is where product recommendation goes: `nav('shop')` is never scoped to a client, so recommending a product means leaving the chart, and no researched product documents a good answer. The nearest model is Practice Better's protocol-to-cart flow with its **"reference only" exclusion toggle** — providers want to recommend things they are not selling — which argues for an in-place picker on the recommendation row rather than a navigation.

## Conclusion

The deepest finding in this research is a category-wide blind spot that happens to be SlickChart's opportunity. Boulevard, Jane, Phorest, Mangomint and GlossGenius all open the chart **from the appointment** and store it **on the client**, which means the client record is designed as a reading surface and an archive — and **not one of the twelve products has a named day-of-appointment mode, a visit checklist, or a "today" panel inside the record**. That design makes sense in a business with a front desk and a calendar someone else manages. It makes no sense for a solo esthetician, for whom the client file is the screen that is actually open, between appointments, on a phone, with five to ten minutes. Everything recommended above is downstream of taking that seriously: a "today" block at the top, the consult as a visit rather than a feature, the camera on the visit rather than in a gallery, and the admin actions moved to where rare things belong.

The second thing worth carrying forward is how little of this is measured. Four findings in this whole set have real effect sizes behind them — focus at **5.56 times the odds** of a usable photo, the **24px floor and 5-to-8mm error cliff** on touch targets, the **57/74/81** attention distribution, and the portal nudge's **10.8% versus 3.9%** — and one more, Baymard's summaries-not-labels, rests on observed behaviour rather than a number. Everything else is vendor documentation, practitioner assertion, or design judgement, and the two most confident-sounding bodies of advice (how many sections a screen may have, and whether capture overlays improve photos) are the two with nothing behind them at all. That argues for a particular kind of restraint: make the changes that are cheap and reversible, instrument the ones that are not, and resist the temptation to settle an unmeasured question by picking the loudest blog. The fastest route to evidence this product can actually trust is the product itself — photo retake rate, summary divergence count, how far down the file a provider actually scrolls — and the client file redesign is the right place to start collecting it.
