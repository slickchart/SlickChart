Every client knows a tattoo is permanent. Fewer know that an ink allergy can appear years later, or that blowout cannot be undone. A tattoo consent form puts all of that on paper while the design is still a stencil.

## Say what the service is

My collection has two general consents: a "Tattoo consent & release" for any kind of work, and a "Standard tattoo consent" for a single-session custom or flash piece. Jobs with their own risks, like a [cover-up](/blog/cover-up-tattoo-consent-form), get their own form.

The standard consent opens with an "about this service" box. Ink goes into the skin with sterile, single-use needles, in one session of about 1 to 5 hours, with stinging or burning that varies by placement. Your client can ask for a break any time. The surface heals in about 2 to 4 weeks, deeper skin in 1 to 3 months.

The release adds how the work is done (single-use needles and ink caps, barrier film on equipment, per local health department rules) and states permanence plainly: laser removal is costly, slow and may not be complete. Your client then checks today's tattoo: custom or flash, black and gray or color, plastic wrap or second-skin film.

## The risks, in plain words

The release lists ten:

- Infection, most often from aftercare problems
- Allergy to ink, red most often, months or years later
- Raised scars and keloids
- Blowout: ink spreading into a blurry halo that cannot be undone
- Bleeding and bruising, more likely with blood thinners or alcohol
- Fading and patchy spots that may need a touch-up
- Granulomas, small bumps around ink
- Rare tingling or burning during an MRI
- Fainting, so eat a meal and drink water first
- Eczema, psoriasis or sun sensitivity flaring in tattooed skin

It closes by logging ink brand, color and lot number for recalls, with US ink reactions reported to the FDA through MedWatch.

## A quick health check

The standard consent has twelve items: pregnancy or breastfeeding, immune suppression or chemo, keloids, eczema or psoriasis, blood thinners or a bleeding disorder, a heart condition or pacemaker, Accutane in the last 6 to 12 months, an allergy to latex, ink or ointment, diabetes, epilepsy or fainting, a sunburn, rash or cut at the site, and alcohol or drugs in the last 24 hours.

A check starts a conversation, never a diagnosis; what a condition means medically is for their doctor to say. The full history lives on the [tattoo intake form](/blog/tattoo-intake-form).

## Design sign-off before the stencil

This is the line I would never cut:

> I approved the final design and stencil placement, and checked every word, name, date and number.

A typo found on the stencil costs a minute. A typo found after costs a cover-up.

## The acknowledgements

- Your client has shared their full health and medication history, including any numbing product applied today.
- The tattoo is permanent, aging, sun and skin can change how it looks, and results are not guaranteed.
- They saw new needles and cartridges opened and ink poured into single-use caps.
- No tattooing over moles, rashes, sunburn or broken skin, so a session may be changed or rescheduled.
- Spreading redness, pus, red streaks or a fever means seeing a doctor and telling the studio.
- Ink brand, color and lot number are logged so they can be contacted about a recall.

Private before and after photos are OK, with social media only by separate release. Then the release itself: your client releases the studio from claims that come from details they withheld or aftercare they skipped, as state law allows. They sign to confirm they are 18 or older with valid photo ID, a copy kept on file, not under the influence, and signing freely.

## Aftercare by stage

My new tattoo guide treats the first weeks as a healing wound:

- **First day:** redness, swelling, and clear plasma with a little ink. Wrap off after 2 to 5 hours, wash with fragrance-free soap, pat dry. No re-wrapping.
- **Days 2 to 3:** wash 2 to 3 times a day, then a very thin layer of ointment.
- **Days 4 to 14:** flaking and itching. Fragrance-free lotion; no picking, soaking or sun.
- **Weeks 2 to 4:** the surface looks healed. SPF 30+ once healed, and no touch-up before 4 to 6 weeks.

The call-me list covers spreading redness after day 3, pus, red streaks, fever or lasting bumps: your client sees a doctor, then texts you so you can log the ink batch. Swelling of the face or throat, or trouble breathing, means calling 911.

## Questions to decide for your own practice

- Where will you log ink brand, color and lot so a recall reaches the right clients?
- Do you check spelling at the consult, on the stencil, or both?
- Tattoo licensing and health department rules vary by state and county, so check with your state and local health department. This post is not legal or medical advice.
