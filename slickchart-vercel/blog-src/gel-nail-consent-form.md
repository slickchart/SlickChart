Gel feels like the gentle option: no liquid and powder, just thin coats and a lamp. So gel clients skim consent forms. The two things that cause real trouble with gel, an allergy that can start after years of wear and nails peeled off at home, only land if she sees them in writing before the first coat.

This exact form is built into [SlickChart](/slickchart): send it to your client's phone to sign before her appointment, and the gel aftercare goes home with her.

## Say what she is booking, in one box

My form opens with a short "about this service" box. Gel polish is a soak-off color, and builder gel is an overlay that strengthens natural nails, both cured under an LED or UV lamp. The visit takes about 60 to 90 minutes: gentle prep, thin coats and a short cure per layer. One line I would never leave out: it should never hurt.

The same box sets the clock. Gel polish lasts about 2 to 3 weeks, builder gel needs an infill every 3 to 4 weeks, and removal is a professional soak-off. Then she checks today's service: gel polish, gel toes, builder gel, builder infill, soak-off or nail art.

## The risks, without the chemistry lecture

My risk list starts with an allergy to acrylates in gel, even after years of use. It then covers irritation around the nail, thinning from picking gel off, a brief heat spike in the lamp while gel cures, itching, redness or blisters near the nails, face or eyelids, and lifting or chipping, especially when nails are used as tools.

Two items tie a habit to a consequence: infection if lifting is ignored and water gets trapped, and UV exposure to the backs of the hands from the lamp.

## Give her a choice about the lamp

Clients ask about lamps. Mine makes it a question she answers, not a warning she skims:

> Lamps cure gel with UV light. Skin cancer risk isn't proven, but SPF 30+ (20 minutes before) or UV-protective fingerless gloves are yours to use.

She picks "SPF or gloves, please" or "No thanks," and her choice is on record.

## A quick health and nail check

Twelve items, each one changing how you work: fungus on nails or skin, eczema or psoriasis on the hands, a dark streak or other nail change, blood thinners or chemo, a sore or swollen nail fold, a past reaction to gel or acrylic, a recent nail injury, light-sensitizing medication, broken skin or warts, an acrylate allergy from dental or medical work, diabetes or poor circulation, and pregnancy or breastfeeding.

A text box underneath lets a checked item come with context. A check starts a conversation, not a verdict, and what a health condition means is for her doctor to say. If she checks a past reaction, my [nail allergy and reaction form](/blog/nail-allergy-reaction-form) post covers the record to keep.

## The promises that protect her nails

Most signature lines are about after she leaves:

- She has shared her full health, medication and nail history, including past reactions to gel, acrylic or nail glue.
- She will speak up about burning or heat in the lamp, or stinging on her skin.
- If itching, redness, swelling or blisters appear, she will stop gel services and see a doctor or dermatologist.
- She will not pick, peel or bite her gel, and will book a fix for lifting within 5 to 7 days instead of gluing or clipping it.

Photos for her private record are required, with social media use only with her separate OK.

## Aftercare that matches the wear cycle

My gel guide follows the weeks:

- **First 24 hours:** cuticle oil tonight, gloves for cleaning and dishes. Skip hot tubs, saunas and long hot soaks.
- **Week 1:** oil daily, hand cream after washing, dry nails well. No opening cans or scraping labels. Her nails are jewels, not tools.
- **Weeks 2 and 3:** regrowth shows at the cuticle. Smooth a snag with a fine file and book the next visit.
- **Fill time:** gel polish is ready to soak off at 2 to 3 weeks, builder gel needs an infill at 3 to 4 weeks. No peeling, prying or clipping at home.

The call-me list covers itching or a rash around the nails, a gap opening under the gel, swelling or blisters on fingertips, face or eyelids, and a green or dark spot under the gel. Swelling of the face or throat, or trouble breathing, means calling 911.

Those weeks double as your rebooking rhythm; see [rebooking for a solo practice](/blog/solo-esthetician-rebooking).

## Questions to decide for your own practice

- Will one gel form cover polish and builder gel, or do you want a fresh form when a client switches?
- When a client checks a past reaction, do you offer a different service or pause until she has seen a dermatologist?
- What does your state board require? Consent rules and scope of practice vary by state, so check with yours. This post is not legal or medical advice.
