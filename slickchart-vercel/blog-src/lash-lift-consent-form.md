A lash lift looks gentle from the outside. No adhesive, nothing added, just the client's own lashes curled. But it is a solution-based service done right at the eye, and two of the ways it can go wrong, over-processing and lifting again too soon, are both things a clear form can help prevent.

## Describe it plainly

My form describes the service in one line: a lifting solution and setting lotion curl the natural lashes over a silicone shield, with tint optional. Then the practical part: about 45 to 75 minutes lying back with eyes closed the whole time, contacts out first, and results that usually last 6 to 8 weeks as new lashes grow in.

The same paragraph sets the spacing rule: wait at least 6 to 8 weeks between lifts.

## Name both kinds of processing risk

Two of the risks are opposites. My list includes:

- **Over-processing:** frizzy, kinked or crimped lashes.
- **Under-processing:** a weak or short-lived curl.
- Lash dryness or breakage, more so if lashes are fine, and an uneven curl between eyes or across the lash line.
- Stinging, redness or watery eyes from the solution, or solution or tint getting into the eye.
- An allergic reaction to lift solution or tint, even later.
- Light skin staining from tint, usually gone in 1 to 2 days.

A client whose curl drops early should already know that was possible: the form says the curl depends on her lashes and their growth stage, and isn't guaranteed.

## Screen for what changes how a lift takes

The health check covers the eye and the lashes themselves: an eye infection, stye or pink eye; eye or eyelid surgery in the past 3 months; extensions on now; a lift or lash perm in the past 6 to 8 weeks; lash serum such as bimatoprost; glaucoma or other eye drops; allergy to tint, PPD or lift solution; contacts; dry eye or blepharitis; rash, eczema or sunburn near the eyes; pregnancy or breastfeeding; chemotherapy; trichotillomania or alopecia; very short, sparse or fragile lashes; and very sensitive or watery eyes.

The serum line is easy to overlook. My aftercare explains why it is there: lash serums with prostaglandins can over-curl lashes. A checked box starts a conversation, and anything that looks like an eye problem gets referred out. This form screens; it does not diagnose.

## Tint as a separate, informed choice

Tint gets its own section, and the client picks either "Tint, please" or "Lift only". The wording above that choice:

> In the US, no color additive is FDA-approved for tinting eyelashes or eyebrows and FDA has reported serious eye injuries from tints, so any tint is used off-label, at my informed choice, after a patch test.

That is how my form words it. Check the FDA's current guidance and your own state board before writing yours. For more on tint wording, see my [lash and brow tint consent form](/blog/lash-brow-tint-consent-form) post.

## The acknowledgements

The client confirms she shared her full health, eye, medication and lash serum history; that she will keep her eyes closed and speak up about stinging or burning; that she had a patch test with no reaction, or declined and signed the waiver; and that she won't lift again sooner than 6 to 8 weeks, because lifting too soon can over-process and break lashes. She also agrees that water, steam or mascara in the first 24 hours can weaken or drop the curl.

## Aftercare built around the first day

The guide opens with: the first 24 hours set your curl. Then:

- **First 24 hours:** keep lashes completely dry, sleep on your back, and skip steam, sweat, mascara and eye rubbing. A very strong curl is normal and softens in a day or two.
- **Days 2 to 7:** brush lashes up each morning and start a non-prostaglandin lash conditioner. No oil-based removers, waterproof mascara or curlers.
- **Weeks 2 to 6:** water-based mascara only, and no harsh scrubs or acids around the eyes.
- **Weeks 6 to 8:** mixed straight and curled lashes are normal. Book the next lift, and no home perm kits.

The call-me list covers eyelid swelling, itching or a rash; redness that gets worse instead of better; discharge, crusting or a gritty feeling; and brittle or breaking lashes. Eye pain or a vision change means urgent eye care, and trouble breathing or face or throat swelling means 911.

That 6 to 8 week rhythm also makes lifts an easy rebook; see [rebooking for solo estheticians](/blog/solo-esthetician-rebooking).

## Questions to decide for your own practice

- Will you lift a client who uses a lash serum, and what will you note in her chart?
- How will you record products and processing time so the next lift is consistent?
- What does your state board require? Consent rules and scope of practice vary by state, so check with yours. This post is not legal or medical advice.
