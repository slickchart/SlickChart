Hot stone feels like pure luxury, and clients book it to melt. The one risk that matters most is simple: a stone that is too hot on skin that cannot warn you. A hot stone consent form names that risk plainly, asks the questions that flag reduced feeling, and gives your client a burn plan before they ever need one.

## What the service is

My form's "about this service" box describes smooth, heated basalt stones that glide with oil as an extension of the therapist's hands. The session runs about 60 to 90 minutes, and every stone is temperature checked before it touches skin. Warmth and relaxation often linger for hours, and light pinkness fades within a few hours.

Your client checks today's session: full body, back, neck and shoulders, an add-on to massage, or cool stones if you offer them.

## How heat is handled

My form explains it in one line:

> Stones are heated in a professional heater, thermometer checked and tested on your therapist's skin first.

Then it lets your client choose: "I prefer mild warmth" or "I prefer it warmer." That choice becomes your starting point next time.

## The possible effects and risks

Eight items, in plain words:

- Skin redness or pinkness that fades within hours
- Feeling too warm, lightheaded or very sleepy
- A skin reaction to oil or lotion, with nut-free on request
- Discomfort from the weight of a placed stone
- A burn or blister if a stone is too hot
- Mild muscle tenderness for a day or two
- Bruising, more likely with blood thinners
- Less heat warning where feeling is reduced

That last line is the reason the health check exists.

## A health check built around heat

Twelve items: diabetes or numbness (neuropathy), high blood pressure, blood thinners, varicose veins, poor circulation, a heart condition, sunburn, rash or a skin condition, recent Botox or fillers, heat sensitivity or MS, pregnancy, recent injury or swelling, and a nut or oil allergy.

A box asks for more about anything checked. A check means a conversation, not a diagnosis, and what a condition means for heat is for their doctor to say. If your client checks pregnancy, my [prenatal massage consent form](/blog/prenatal-massage-consent-form) post covers the form for that.

## The acknowledgements

These lines run both ways:

- Your client has shared their health history, including diabetes, circulation, heat sensitivity and medications.
- They understand the risks, including the small chance of a burn if a stone is too hot.
- They will tell you right away if any stone feels too hot, uncomfortable or too heavy.
- Placed stones rest on a sheet or towel and are never left under their body on bare skin.
- They can ask for less heat, change pressure or stop at any time.
- Sensitive areas are worked only with their prior OK, which they can withdraw.
- They will check their skin afterward and report any blister, burn or redness that does not fade.

The placed stone line tells your client what you do, not just what they agree to.

## Aftercare, including the burn check

My hot stone guide follows the rest of the day:

- **First few hours:** skin may look pink or flushed where stones were, and they may feel warm and sleepy. Sip water, get up slowly, cool down gradually, and do not drive if lightheaded.
- **Rest of the day:** pinkness fades within a few hours and does not hurt. Loose clothing and a lukewarm shower to remove oil. No hot tubs, saunas, sunbathing or heating pads on warm skin.
- **Days 1 and 2:** mild muscle tenderness can happen, then eases. Gentle stretching, walking, rest, and ice or warmth, whichever feels good.

Then comes a burn check. Redness that stings, lingers or blisters is a burn, not normal pinkness. The guide says to cool it under cool, not icy, running water for 20 minutes, then call you, and to avoid ice right on skin, popping blisters, or butter and greasy creams.

The good-to-know list adds that normal pinkness is even, painless and fades in hours, that stones were washed and disinfected before use, and that anyone with reduced feeling in an area should check that skin tonight. The call-me list covers a blister, skin that is white, shiny or very sore, swelling or pain that gets worse, redness still there the next day, and dizziness that does not pass. Large, deep or facial burns, chest pain or trouble breathing means calling 911.

## Questions to decide for your own practice

- Will you offer cool stones or a non-heated massage when a client checks diabetes, neuropathy or heat sensitivity?
- Where will you record the stone temperature and your client's warmth preference each visit?
- What does your state board require? Consent rules, sanitation rules and scope of practice vary by state, so check with yours. This post is not legal or medical advice.
