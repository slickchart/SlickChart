## Say what fine line work is, and how it ages

My "about this service" box describes single-needle or fine-line work and small designs, often under 2 inches, with fine detail. The session usually runs 30 to 90 minutes, and lines go in slowly with a light hand to help keep them crisp.

Then the line that matters most: fine lines soften, blur and fade over the years more than bold work, and a touch-up is often needed. Your client checks today's tattoo: single needle, fine line, micro, script, dotwork, or touch-up booked.

## The risks, written for thin lines

- Blowout: ink spreading into a blur that cannot be undone
- Patchy or broken lines after healing
- Tiny details merging together over time
- Allergy to ink, or infection, as with any tattoo
- Lines softening, blurring or spreading as skin ages
- Faster fading on hands, fingers, feet and ribs
- Quick fading if the tattoo gets too much sun
- Rare burning or tingling during an MRI scan

Most of these are about time, not the session. For the general risks, see my [tattoo consent form](/blog/tattoo-consent-form) post.

## A health and skin check for fine work

Twelve items. Two are specific to fine line: moles in the design area, and thin or sun-damaged skin. The rest cover pregnancy or breastfeeding, immune suppression or chemo, Accutane in the last 6 to 12 months, blood thinners or a bleeding disorder, a sunburn, tan or rash at the site, an allergy to latex, ink or ointment, diabetes or slow healing, keloids, eczema or psoriasis, and a heart condition or epilepsy.

A check starts a conversation, never a diagnosis; what a skin condition means medically is for their doctor to say.

## The size and placement choice

This is the part I would never cut. The form says very small text and detail blur together as skin ages, and your artist may suggest a larger size or a spot that holds fine work better. Then your client picks one of two answers:

> Keeping my size; I accept the risk

The other option is "Going with the suggested size." Either way, the choice is on record.

## The acknowledgements

- Your client has shared their full health and medication history, including any numbing product applied today.
- Fine lines can blur, spread and fade over time, and crisp healed lines are not guaranteed.
- They approved the final design, size and placement, and checked all spelling, names, dates and numbers.
- A touch-up is often needed, and it is done no sooner than 4 to 6 weeks.
- Hands, fingers, feet and ribs fade faster.
- They will care for it gently with a thin layer of ointment, keep it out of the sun, then use SPF 30+ once healed.
- Ink brand, color and lot number are logged so they can be contacted about a recall.

Photos for their private record are OK, with social media only by separate release. Your client signs to confirm they are 18 or older with valid photo ID, a copy kept on file, not under the influence, and consenting to this fine line tattoo.

## Aftercare that uses less of everything

My fine line guide starts from one idea: fine lines hold less ink than bold work, so they need the gentlest care.

- **First day:** lines can look slightly fuzzy or raised. Wrap off at 2 to 5 hours, and wash softly with fingertips and fragrance-free soap. No washcloths or loofahs.
- **Days 2 to 3:** lines may look thicker from swelling, and this settles. Pat dry, and a rice-grain amount of ointment only if it feels dry. Heavy ointment softens lines.
- **Days 4 to 14:** light flaking, and lines can look patchy. Thin fragrance-free lotion; no picking, soaking or sun.
- **Weeks 3 to 6:** lines settle crisp and fine, and small gaps may show where ink did not hold. SPF 30+ once healed, and a photo to you at 4 to 6 weeks if there are gaps.

The guide is direct: UV is the biggest reason fine lines fade, and too much moisture blurs them.

The call-me list covers spreading redness after day 3, pus, red streaks, fever or lasting bumps: your client sees a doctor, then texts you so you can log the ink batch. Swelling of the face or throat, or trouble breathing, means calling 911.
