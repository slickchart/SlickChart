"It's just a manicure" is easy to think, and easy to skip a consent form over. But a manicure with an e-file is precise work at the cuticle line, with a spinning bit close to living skin. A short form sets clear expectations about what is removed, what is never cut, and what happens if a tiny nick does occur.

## Describe the dry manicure

My form calls the service nail shaping, cuticle care and her choice of finish, with an e-file that may be used to refine cuticles. It takes about 30 to 60 minutes, and it explains that a dry, no-soak manicure uses an e-file instead of a soak.

It also gives her a realistic timeline. Neat cuticles last about 2 to 3 weeks and polish about 5 to 7 days, and the form suggests booking every 2 to 4 weeks. She checks today's service: natural manicure, cuticle care, e-file (dry) manicure or polish.

## Be exact about cuticles

The cuticle rule is the heart of this form, and it is stated in the risks and again in the acknowledgements:

> Only loose, non-living cuticle is removed; living skin is never cut. Rules on cutting vary by state.

That second sentence matters. What you may cut, and with what, is a state board question, so check your own state's rules before you copy anyone's wording, including mine.

## The risk list

The rest of the risks:

- Small nicks or tenderness at the cuticle line.
- Infection if skin is broken and not kept clean.
- Brief warmth or vibration from the e-file.
- An allergic reaction to an oil, lotion or polish.
- Redness or a sore nail fold for a day or two.
- Thin or heat-sensitive nails if overfiled.
- Hangnails if cuticles are then cut at home.
- Results that vary with thick or very dry cuticles.

## The health and nail check

Fifteen items: diabetes, chemo or immune suppression, a red or sore nail fold, lifting or a recent nail injury, gel or acrylic recently removed, blood thinners, pregnancy, nail fungus or warts, a dark streak or nail change, an allergy to nail products, poor circulation or neuropathy, cuts, hangnails or torn skin, eczema or psoriasis on the hands, very thin or peeling nails, and biting nails or picking cuticles.

A text box lets her explain any item. A check is a reason to talk and adjust, never a diagnosis, and anything medical belongs with her doctor.

## What she agrees to

The acknowledgements make the e-file part explicit:

- She has shared her full health history, including diabetes, blood thinners and skin conditions.
- Only loose, non-living cuticle is removed, and living skin is never cut.
- She agrees to e-file use by a trained nail artist, with a disinfected bit or a new sanding band.
- She will speak up right away if anything feels hot, stings, pinches or hurts, so you can stop.
- A small nick can happen. It will be cleaned and covered, and she will watch it for signs of infection.
- She will follow aftercare, use cuticle oil daily, and not cut or pick her cuticles at home.

Deposits, fees and fixes follow your policy, and photos for her private record are OK, with social media only with her separate OK.

The nick line is the one I would never drop: it turns a rare moment into a known step.

## Aftercare for natural nails

My guide follows the regrowth:

- **Today:** skin may look a little pink or tender. Cuticle oil tonight, gloves for dishes and cleaning, and let polish set fully.
- **Days 1 to 7:** polish is fully hard after about 24 hours. Oil daily, hand cream after washing, no biting or cutting cuticles.
- **Weeks 2 and 3:** after a shower, push cuticles back gently with a towel. No clippers on living skin, no peeling polish.
- **2 to 4 weeks:** book the next manicure. Waiting too long lets dry, overgrown cuticles split into hangnails.

The guide explains that the cuticle seals the base of the nail against germs, and asks her to leave any trimming to you. Its call-me list covers redness, swelling or warmth around a nail, red streaks from a finger, throbbing, pus or worsening pain, and a nick that has not healed in a few days. For a client with diabetes or poor circulation, a slow-healing wound means seeing her doctor the same day.

A 2 to 4 week rhythm makes rebooking simple; see [rebooking for a solo practice](/blog/solo-esthetician-rebooking).

## Questions to decide for your own practice

- Which bits will you use at the cuticle, and how will you record bit and band changes?
- Will you e-file a client who checked blood thinners, or keep her service to a natural manicure?
- What does your state board require? Cuticle cutting rules, e-file rules and scope of practice vary by state, so check with yours. This post is not legal or medical advice.
