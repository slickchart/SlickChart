Sooner or later a client says, "I've worn gel for years and never had a problem," and then shows you itchy, red fingertips. Her history is the clue, not the reassurance. Allergies to nail products can start after years of safe use, and a short, dedicated allergy and reaction record is how you catch the pattern before it gets worse.

This exact form is built into [SlickChart](/slickchart): send it to your client's phone to fill in and sign before her first visit, and her nail care guide goes home with her.

## Why a separate allergy record

Your service consent forms already ask about past reactions in their health checks. A separate record goes further. It keeps the full story in one place, signed and dated, so every future service starts from it. Mine opens with a short explanation she reads and confirms: gel, acrylic, dip and nail glue contain acrylates. An allergy can develop after years of safe use, usually when uncured product touches skin, and once it starts it is lifelong. It can also affect future dental work and medical devices.

That last point is the one that makes clients pay attention. It is not about her next set. It is about her next filling.

## Stay inside your scope

The same box draws a clear line about testing:

> Nail techs do not do acrylate patch tests: patch testing is done by a dermatologist.

This is worth saying in writing. A client who has had a reaction may ask you to "just test a nail." The form answers for you: identifying an allergy is a dermatologist's job, and your job is to record what happened and not put product on skin that has reacted. Nothing on this form, and nothing in this post, is a diagnosis.

## The four questions

The core of the record is four yes or no questions:

1. Has she ever reacted to gel, acrylic, dip or nail glue?
2. Has she had itching, redness or swelling after a past nail service?
3. Has she reacted to dental work, dentures or lash glue?
4. Has a dermatologist diagnosed her with an acrylate allergy?

The third question surprises people, but the form already told her an acrylate allergy can reach dental work, so a reaction there is worth knowing about before you start. A details box underneath asks where, when and what happened.

Above the questions are two short fields that are easy to overlook: known allergies, and her dermatologist and clinic. If a reaction ever happens, you both know who she should call.

## How it fits with intake

My nail intake asks the broader allergy question as a checklist: acrylates (gel, acrylic, dip), nail glue or adhesives, latex, fragrance, nickel or other metals, acetone or removers, dental or medical acrylic, and lotions or skin products, with "none that I know of" as an option. The intake also has her acknowledge that nail product allergies can start at any time, even after years of safe use, and that she will report any itching or redness.

The allergy record is where a "yes" on that checklist becomes a full account. My pre-visit guide even asks: ever had itching or a rash from nail products? Tell me before you book. So the question reaches her twice before she sits down.

## What she agrees to

The record closes with one acknowledgement and a signature. She confirms she has shared her allergy history honestly, that she will report any itching, redness or swelling, and that she will call 911 for trouble breathing.

The service consents back this up. My gel and acrylic forms have her agree that if itching, redness, swelling or blisters appear, she stops those services and sees a doctor or dermatologist. See how that reads in context in my [gel nail consent form](/blog/gel-nail-consent-form) post.

## What the aftercare tells her

My general nail care guide puts the next steps in plain words. Call if she notices itching, redness or a rash near her nails, eyelids or face. Possible allergy? Have the product removed, see a doctor, and tell me. It ends with the reminder that allergies can start after years of safe use, and that trouble breathing or a swollen face or throat means calling 911. It also covers product in the eye: flush for 15 minutes, then get urgent care.

Notice the order. The tech removes the product, the doctor assesses, and the record gets updated. Each person stays in their own role.
