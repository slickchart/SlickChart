"I've had my color done for years, I don't need a test." Every stylist hears it. The trouble is that a dye allergy does not care how long someone has been coloring. A short, separate testing form gives you a calm, written way to offer the test every time, and a clear record when a client says no.

## Why it is its own form

My hair collection has a full chemical service consent, plus a consent for each service. The allergy alert and strand test form sits beside them, on purpose. The test happens 48 hours before the service, so it needs its own date, its own choice and its own signature.

It collects only what matters for testing: her phone number, the planned service, known allergies, the appointment date, and one prompt that is easy to skip on a longer intake: "including any black henna tattoo."

## Explain why you test

The form opens with a "why we test" box, and the client confirms she has read it. In part:

> Most hair colors contain PPD, PTD or related dyes, and allergy can develop at any time, even after years of safe use.

It says the salon tests 48 hours before every color service, or as the manufacturer directs. That second clause matters. Testing method and timing belong to the product you use, so follow its instructions, and check what your state board expects.

## The strand test is a different question

The same box explains the second test. An allergy alert test is about the client's skin; a strand test shows how her hair lifts and holds up. The form calls a strand test a must if box dye, henna or "progressive" dye may be present.

My chemical service consent explains why that history is worth asking about: box dye, henna or metallic dyes can react with peroxide and break hair. And my "before your salon visit" guide asks clients to mention box dye, henna or at-home color from the past 2 years, even if it has grown out.

## Consent or decline, nothing in between

The client picks one: "I consent to these tests" or "I decline testing."

If she consents, she agrees to a simple plan. If the spot burns, blisters or itches badly, she will rinse it and call you. For trouble breathing or face or throat swelling, she will call 911.

If she declines, she checks which test she is turning down, the allergy alert test, the strand test or both, and signs a short waiver. It says she was offered the tests and chose not to have them; that an allergy or poor hair reaction may not be found first and could cause swelling, breakage, an unwanted result or a need for medical care; and that she accepts those risks and releases your business from related claims as far as state law allows.

That last phrase is doing honest work. A waiver is not a shield against everything, and how far it reaches depends on where you practice. Ask your state board, or a local attorney, before relying on one.

## How it connects to the service forms

Each service consent asks for the test result again, so nothing slips through. My color consent records "Test done, no reaction" or "Declined, waiver signed," and repeats that PPD allergy can develop at any time. The lightening form asks about an allergy alert test for any toner or color. The chemical service consent confirms the test or waiver, and agreement to a strand test when recommended.

The pre-visit guide closes the loop for the client. Book the test 2 weeks ahead if she needs one; 48 hours before, the test should be done and checked for itching or redness; and any itching or redness from the test is a reason to call and reschedule. For more on each service, see my [hair color consent form](/blog/hair-color-consent-form) and [hair lightening consent form](/blog/hair-lightening-consent-form) posts.

## Stay inside your scope

A reaction at a test spot is a reason to stop and reschedule, not something to diagnose. My chemical service consent has the client agree to contact you, and a healthcare provider, if signs of a reaction appear. The form's own wording keeps you there: rinse, call, and 911 for trouble breathing.

## Questions to decide for your own practice

- Will you ever color a client who declines the allergy alert test?
- How often will you re-test regulars, and where will you note the date and result?
- What does your state board require? Rules vary by state, so check with yours. This post is not legal or medical advice.
