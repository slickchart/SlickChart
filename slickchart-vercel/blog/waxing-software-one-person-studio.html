What to look for in waxing software when you're a one-person shop

October 19, 2026 · 5 min read

Most waxing software is sold on booking. Online scheduling, deposits, reminders. Those matter, but they are not what goes wrong in a waxing room.

What goes wrong is the client who started a retinoid three weeks ago and didn't think to mention it. Or the regular whose skin lifted last time, and you can't remember which area or which wax. Or the brow client you meant to rebook at four weeks who drifted to eight.

If you work alone, there is no front desk to catch those things. Your software has to. Here is what to look for, whatever you end up using.

1. An intake form that asks the right questions by name

A generic "any medical conditions?" box gets a generic "no." The questions that protect a waxing client are specific, and they work better when they name the products:

  • Oral isotretinoin (Accutane), and when they last took it
  • Topical retinoids such as tretinoin, adapalene or tazarotene
  • AHAs, BHAs or other exfoliants used in the last few days
  • Recent peels, sunburn or tanning
  • Blood thinners and other medications that change how skin bruises or heals

Decide ahead of time what a "yes" means for each one: go ahead, skip that area, or postpone. The waiting periods themselves come from your training, your state board and the client's prescriber, not from your software. Sources disagree on some of them, so pick yours deliberately and write them down.

What software can do is make sure the answers reach you before the client is on the table, and make a "yes" impossible to miss. Look for forms the client fills out ahead of time, with flagged answers called out on their own instead of buried in a PDF.

2. A check before every visit, not just the first

An intake form is a snapshot. Your June client may have started a prescription acne treatment in August. If the only time you asked was her first visit, you won't know.

The best fix is a short check-in that goes out before every appointment. Three questions are enough: any skin changes, any new medications, any new products. "No changes" takes the client five seconds. A "yes" gives you time to adjust before she arrives.

When you compare tools, ask: can it send a pre-visit check-in on its own, every time, without me remembering to?

3. Notes that are about the skin, not just the appointment

A booking app records that a Brazilian happened on Tuesday. A waxing chart records how it went:

  • Which wax, and anything you changed (temperature, pre-wax oil, a different wax for a sensitive area)
  • Areas done, and any you skipped and why
  • Reactions: redness that lingered, lifting, bruising, ingrown hairs, breakouts
  • What you told the client to do afterwards

Next time, that note is the first thing you should see. Look for software where notes sit on the client's record and are quick to add with wet or gloved hands. If it takes six taps, you'll stop doing it by Thursday. We go deeper on this in what to record in a client chart.

4. Photos that don't live in your camera roll

Photos help track a reaction or an ingrown area over time. They are also sensitive, especially for body waxing. Take them only with the client's written consent, and keep them out of your personal camera roll.

Ask any vendor: where are client photos stored, who can see them, and can I delete them?

5. Aftercare the client can find again

Most of what happens after a wax happens at home. Tight clothing, heat, exfoliating too soon. A verbal "no hot tubs for a day or two" is forgotten by the parking lot.

Look for a way to send aftercare in writing that the client can open again on day three, when the ingrown question comes up.

6. Rebooking that follows the hair, not the calendar

Waxing has a natural rhythm. The right next appointment depends on the area and the client's regrowth. The habit that works is booking the next visit before they leave the room. A tool that lets you book in a couple of taps, while the client is still standing there, earns its keep. There's more on that habit in rebooking for solo pros.

7. Don't rip out what already works

Here's the question most software comparisons skip: do you actually need to replace your booking system?

If your booking and payments already run on Square, moving everything to a new all-in-one means rebuilding your services, retraining your regulars and hoping nothing gets lost along the way. Sometimes that's worth it. Often, what's really missing is the charting, forms and aftercare, not the booking.

That gap is why we built SlickChart. It connects to the Square account you already have, so your bookings and clients come in and you don't start over. It adds the parts above: intake and consent forms with flagged answers shown before the visit, a pre-visit check-in that sends itself, notes and photos on the client's record, and a private client app for aftercare and messages.

To be fair to the all-in-ones: if you're opening a multi-room waxing studio from scratch, with staff and a front desk, a full salon platform built around that may suit you better. Whatever you're looking at, ask these questions before you sign:

  • Do clients fill out intake before the visit, and are risky answers flagged?
  • Does it re-check health and medication changes before every appointment?
  • Can I chart reactions per area, quickly, with gloves on?
  • Where do photos live, and can I delete them?
  • How does the client get aftercare after they leave?
  • Can I export all of my client records if I ever leave?

Software won't make the call on the table. That's yours. But it can make sure you have the right information before you get there.
