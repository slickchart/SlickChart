Saline tattoo removal consent form: realistic goals and dry healing

October 19, 2026 · 5 min read

A client booking saline removal arrives unhappy with work she already has, and she may be hoping one visit will make it disappear. The consent form's main job is to replace that hope with a plan she has agreed to in writing.

This exact form is built into SlickChart: send it to your client's phone to sign before the visit, and the aftercare below goes home with her.

Explain the method, then lower the bar

My form explains saline in one sentence: saline solution is worked into old pigment with a sterile, single-use needle, and the pigment lifts out with the scab. It says it is best for lightening PMU.

Then it sets realistic expectations in three parts. Usually 2 to 4 or more sessions, at least 8 weeks apart, on fully healed tattoos only. The area scabs and stays dry for 7 to 10 days. And the line I want every removal client to read twice:

Each session lightens; it rarely erases in one visit.

The box finishes by saying some pigment may not lift fully and complete removal is not guaranteed.

Agree on the goal for today and for the plan

Removal clients want different things, so my form asks. The "area and goal" section outlines the exact area to lighten and lets you choose together: lighten for a correction, lighten for a cover, lighten as much as possible, or one section only. She approves the area on its own line, and there is space for notes.

That choice matters later. A client who signed "lighten for a cover" is not measuring today against bare skin.

Risks of lifting pigment

Saline has its own risk profile, and the form names it:

  • incomplete removal or uneven lightening
  • scarring or texture change, more likely with picking
  • healing that takes longer than expected
  • darker marks, more likely in deeper skin tones
  • lighter patches of skin (hypopigmentation)
  • infection, especially if the scab gets wet or picked
  • a color shift as pigment lifts, toward warm, red or orange tones
  • allergy, or temporary hair loss in brow areas

The color shift line is the one that saves a panicked message. Old brow pigment turning warm mid-process looks alarming if nobody warned her.

Screening for timing as much as health

Two items on the checklist are about the tattoo itself: a tattoo less than 8 weeks old or not healed, and laser or other removal in the past 8 weeks. The rest cover pregnancy, keloids, Accutane in the past 6 months, blood thinners, an open wound or rash in the area, slow healing, chemo or radiation, and allergies. A free text box follows. A check is a reason to talk, not a diagnosis.

Acknowledgements built around the scab

She confirms that several sessions at least 8 weeks apart are usually needed and complete removal is not promised. She agrees to keep the area dry with no ointment for 7 to 10 days and let the scab fall off on its own. She accepts that new permanent makeup over the area waits until it is fully healed and you approve it. And she agrees that before photos are taken each session so progress is tracked honestly. Those photos are your best friend when a client feels nothing has changed.

Aftercare that breaks the usual rules

Most PMU aftercare says balm. Saline says the opposite, and my guide explains why: the scab is doing the work, so keeping the area clean and completely dry is what makes it succeed.

  • Day 0: blot with clean, dry gauze until weeping stops, and leave it uncovered unless you say otherwise. No water, ointment, balm or makeup.
  • Days 1 to 3: a dry crust forms with pigment inside it. Wash around it and shower with her face away from the water.
  • Days 4 to 10: the scab tightens, itches and falls off on its own. Picking or soaking it off pulls pigment out unevenly and can scar.
  • Weeks 2 to 4: pink, shiny new skin. No sun, tanning, retinoids or acids for 4 weeks.
  • Week 8 and later: book the next session at least 8 weeks out, and no new PMU on the area until you have checked it.

The call-me list includes a scab that turns soft, wet or yellow. If the area was near the eyes, eye pain, vision changes or discharge means urgent eye care, and trouble breathing or facial swelling means 911.

Questions to decide for your own practice

  • Will you remove another artist's work? My booking policy asks for a consultation and photos first, and notes that correcting another artist's work may need lightening or removal before new work.
  • How will you compare session photos side by side? (See what to record in a client chart.)
  • When will you book the cover work: only after healing and your own check?
  • PMU licensing, health department and bloodborne pathogen rules vary by state and county, so check with your state and local health department and board. This post is not legal or medical advice.
