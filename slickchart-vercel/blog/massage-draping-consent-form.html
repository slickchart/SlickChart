Massage draping consent form: area by area consent, boundaries and physician clearance

October 19, 2026 · 5 min read

Most clients will never say out loud that they are nervous about undressing for a massage, or that they do not want their glutes or abdomen touched. A draping consent form lets them say it on paper, before the session, with no awkward conversation. It also puts your professional boundaries in writing, which protects you as much as it reassures them.

This exact form is built into SlickChart: send it to your client's phone to sign before their first session, and their area choices are in their chart every time they book.

Explain draping before anything else

My form opens with a short "how draping works" box. Your client undresses to their comfort level in private, then lies under a sheet or blanket. Only the area being worked on is uncovered, then covered again. Genitals and the gluteal cleft are always draped, breasts stay draped per state rules, and your client is welcome to stay partly or fully clothed.

That last sentence does a lot of work for a nervous first-timer.

Area by area consent

This is the heart of the form. For each area, your client picks Yes, No or Ask first:

  • Scalp
  • Face: jaw, forehead, around the eyes
  • Neck, with the front of the throat avoided
  • Chest wall: pectorals, above breast tissue
  • Abdomen: gentle, clockwise strokes
  • Glutes, over the drape
  • Upper inner thigh, never near the groin
  • Feet
  • Hands

Writing the boundary into the area name, such as "over the drape" or "never near the groin," tells your client exactly what a Yes means. "Ask first" gives a cautious client a middle option instead of a hard No. A notes box underneath collects any other areas to avoid.

Comfort choices that make the session theirs

The second half reads like a preference card: pressure from 1 very light to 5 very firm, oil or lotion (standard oil, nut-free, unscented, lotion or no product), areas to focus on and areas to skip entirely. Then the room itself: music on, soft or off; talking as chat is fine, quiet, or check-ins only; the door locked or unlocked; and temperature warmer, cooler or a heated table.

An optional question asks about therapist gender preference, and an optional box asks:

Anything else that helps you feel safe and comfortable?

The boundary line

The form closes with a plain statement of professional conduct: genital contact is never part of massage, and the session ends if behavior is inappropriate, from either side. Your client can change their consent at any time, before or during the session, just by saying so, and the form promises you will always ask.

"From either side" is worth keeping. It tells your client the rule protects them too, not only you.

The general massage consent in the same set repeats the core of this in one line: only the area being worked is uncovered, and your client can stay clothed. It also states that sexual contact is never part of massage. Each specialty consent, such as my deep tissue massage consent form, adds that sensitive areas are worked only with your client's OK first.

When a doctor needs to weigh in: physician clearance

Some health answers on intake mean massage should wait, or be adjusted, until your client's health care provider agrees. My physician clearance form is a short, separate page for that moment.

It asks for name, date of birth and phone, then the reason clearance is requested: a high-risk pregnancy, recent surgery, cosmetic surgery before lymphatic drainage, cancer now or in treatment, a heart or circulation condition, or other. There is a line for weeks pregnant, a box for the condition or procedure and its date, and a checklist of the services requested: Swedish, deep tissue, prenatal, hot stone, cupping or lymphatic drainage.

The authorization is the important part. Your client authorizes their provider to share the form with your business, it shares only what they authorize and only for planning massage care, and they can withdraw it in writing at any time.

What a condition means for massage is for their doctor to say. The form just makes that conversation easy to have and to file. For how it fits a pregnancy, see my prenatal massage consent form post.

Before the first visit

My "before your massage" guide repeats the draping promise in the client's own words: they undress to their comfort level in private, rest under a sheet, only the area being worked is uncovered, and they can speak up, stay clothed or stop at any time. Sending that ahead of the visit means the form is not the first time they hear it.

Questions to decide for your own practice

  • Will you ask clients to review their area choices each visit, or only when something changes?
  • How will you handle an "Ask first" area mid-session, so the question feels natural and not awkward?
  • What does your state board require? Draping rules, consent rules and scope of practice vary by state, so check with yours. This post is not legal or medical advice.
