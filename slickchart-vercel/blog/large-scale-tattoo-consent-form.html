Large-scale tattoo consent form: sleeves, color realism and the multi-session plan

October 19, 2026 · 5 min read

A sleeve is not one appointment. It is a plan that runs across months, and the client who signs on for it needs to know that an unfinished piece looks unfinished, that long sessions are hard on the body, and that each area has to heal before you touch it again. A large-scale consent form puts that plan on paper.

This exact form is built into SlickChart: send it to your client's phone to sign before each session, and the large tattoo aftercare goes home with them.

Say what the work is, and what it asks of them

My "about this service" box covers sleeves, back pieces and detailed color or realism work, built over several sessions from one design. Sessions run 3 to 6 hours. Your client eats a full meal, drinks water and sleeps well first, and breaks are built in. Each area heals 3 to 4 weeks before it is worked on again, and the session count is an estimate.

Two short lines record the estimated sessions and today's session number, out of how many.

The risks, scaled up

  • Swelling, bruising and soreness across a large area
  • A longer, itchier heal, often 3 to 6 weeks per area
  • Color fading faster in sun, and uneven healing
  • Raised scars or keloids, mainly in scar-prone skin
  • Fatigue, light-headedness or fainting in long sessions
  • Allergy to ink, most often red, even years later
  • Infection if a large area is not kept clean
  • Rare burning or tingling during an MRI scan

For the general risks, see my tattoo consent form post.

A health check for long sessions

Twelve items, including two that matter more over 3 to 6 hours: low blood sugar or anemia, and epilepsy or fainting. The rest cover pregnancy or breastfeeding, immune suppression or chemo, an allergy to red ink, latex or ointment, blood thinners or a bleeding disorder, a heart condition or pacemaker, keloids, a sunburn, rash or cut on the area, diabetes, Accutane in the last 6 to 12 months, and alcohol or drugs in the last 24 hours.

A check starts a conversation, never a diagnosis; what a condition means medically is for their doctor to say.

Numbing over a big area

Numbing follows your studio policy, and the form passes on a warning:

The FDA warns against lidocaine over 4 percent or heavy use over large areas.

Your client then confirms they have shared any numbing product applied today along with their health history.

The acknowledgements

  • Your client approved the overall design and placement and checked all spelling, and later changes may add sessions.
  • The session count is an estimate, and if they stop early, the piece may look unfinished.
  • They will let each area heal 3 to 4 weeks between sessions and follow aftercare after every session.
  • They will eat, hydrate and rest before each session, and tell you right away if they feel faint or unwell.
  • Ink brand, color and lot number are logged each session so they can be contacted about a recall.

Before and progress photos for their record are OK, with social media only by separate release. Your client signs to confirm they are 18 or older with valid photo ID, a copy kept on file, not under the influence, and consenting to this multi-session plan.

Aftercare for a big heal

My large tattoo guide opens by saying big, color-packed pieces ask more of the body: more swelling, a longer heal, and each session healed before the next.

  • First day: big areas swell, ooze and feel sore like a sunburn. Wrap off at 2 to 5 hours, wash with fragrance-free soap, rest and drink water. No alcohol or hard workouts.
  • Days 2 to 4: swelling, warmth and stiffness peak, mostly on legs and arms. Raise a swollen limb and use very thin ointment for 3 to 4 days. No tight sleeves, waistbands or straps.
  • Days 5 to 21: peeling and itching over a big area, and color looks milky. Thin fragrance-free lotion and loose, soft clothing. No scratching, pools, baths, hot tubs, the sea or sun.
  • Weeks 3 to 6: the surface heals but stays shiny longer. SPF 30+ once healed, because color fades fast in UV, and confirm the next session.

Before the next session, your client tells you about new medications, illness or sunburn, and arrives rested, fed and hydrated.

The call-me list covers spreading redness after day 3, pus, red streaks, fever or lasting bumps: your client sees a doctor, then texts you so you can log the ink batch. Swelling of the face or throat, or trouble breathing, means calling 911.

Questions to decide for your own practice

  • How long a session will you book for a first-timer, and how will you handle breaks?
  • Where will you track which session of the plan each visit is?
  • Tattoo licensing and health department rules vary by state and county, so check with your state and local health department. This post is not legal or medical advice.
