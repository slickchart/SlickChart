Sooner or later, something you have to ask, record or wait for will change. Your state board updates a rule. Your insurer asks for a new line on your consent form. A product you use comes with new instructions for patch testing. None of it is dramatic, but all of it has to show up in how you book and check in your clients.

What happens next depends on who owns your software.

## When the software is rented

On a platform built for everyone, your forms and booking rules are whatever the platform allows. Some of that you can edit yourself, and a good platform makes forms easy to change. But the deeper parts, like a waiting period between two services, a question that blocks a booking until it is answered, or a rule that only applies to one treatment, are usually features. If the feature is not there, you ask for it and wait, or you work around it.

The work-around is where things slip. A sticky note by the treatment chair. A question you ask out loud and hope to remember. A rule that lives in your head instead of in the system, which means it is only followed on the days you remember it.

That is not a criticism of the platforms. They serve thousands of businesses at once, and one board's rule in one state is not going to move their roadmap. It is just the trade you make when the software belongs to someone else.

## When the app is yours

When you own your app, a rule change is something you describe, not something you request.

You write down what changed, in plain words, the way you would explain it to a new assistant: what the client must be asked, which services it applies to, what should happen if the answer is no. Then you ask the AI to make that change, and you test it. That is the whole loop. The code is not the hard part anymore, and a rule change is exactly the kind of small, clear request AI handles well. I wrote more about describing changes in [how to describe what you want built](/build/blog/describing-what-you-want-built).

It also means the rule lives in the app, not in your memory. If a service needs a patch test on file, the app can refuse the booking until there is one, every time, including the busy Saturday when you would have forgotten. That idea gets its own post in [the booking your app should refuse](/build/blog/booking-your-app-should-refuse).

## The honest trade-off: you are the one who checks

Owning the app means owning the job of keeping it current. Nobody else is watching your board's updates on your behalf. That is real responsibility, and it is worth being clear-eyed about it.

A few habits make it manageable:

- **Know where the rules come from.** Your state board, your insurer, and the manufacturers of the products you use. Bookmark each one's page and check them on a schedule you choose.
- **Keep a short list of what your app enforces and why.** One line per rule, with where it came from. When a source changes, you know exactly which part of the app to change.
- **Change one thing at a time.** Describe one rule, test it, then move to the next. A batch of changes at once is harder to check.
- **Test it as a client.** Book the affected service on your own phone. Try to break the rule on purpose. If it lets you through, it is not done.
- **Keep what was signed.** A client who signed last year's consent form signed last year's wording. Your app should keep that version with their record, and show the new one to the next client.

None of this needs you to read code. It needs you to know your trade, which you already do. If you want a reminder of how to check an app you did not write line by line, see [testing an app when you cannot read code](/build/blog/testing-an-app-you-cannot-read).

## Why this matters more than it sounds

Most rule changes are small. A new question, a new line, a longer wait between two services. The risk is never the change itself. It is the gap between when the rule changes and when your system catches up, because that gap is filled by memory.

With your own app, that gap is as short as you make it. You decide when the change goes in, how it works, and what your clients see. Check your own board for the rules that apply to you, since they differ from state to state.
