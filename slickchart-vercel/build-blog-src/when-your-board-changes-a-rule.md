Every licensed beauty pro knows the feeling. A notice arrives, a class mentions it, or a colleague posts about it: something about how you are expected to work has changed. A record you now need to keep. A question you now need to ask before a service. A form that needs a new line.

Updating your own habits is the easy part. The harder question is whether your software can follow.

## What changes, and how often

It is not only boards. The things that shape how you work change from several directions at once:

- **Your state board** updates rules on records, sanitation, consent or what a license covers.
- **Your insurer** asks for something new on intake or consent before they will cover a service.
- **A product manufacturer** changes its instructions, a patch test window or a contraindication list.
- **Your own policies** change: deposits, cancellations, who you will and will not treat.

None of these are rare over the life of a business. And each one usually lands somewhere in your software: a form, a question, a booking rule, a reminder.

Whatever the specific change, always read it from the source itself, your board, your insurer or the manufacturer, rather than a summary. This post is about what happens after you know what changed, not about what any rule says.

## When the software is rented

With software built for everyone, a change to your work becomes a request to someone else's roadmap.

Sometimes the change is something you can do yourself: edit a form, add a question. That is the good case. But when the change touches how the app behaves, such as a new rule about when a service can be booked, a record that has to be kept for every visit, or a question that must be answered before a specific treatment, you are often left with a workaround. A note in the booking page. A paper form alongside the digital one. A reminder to yourself to check by hand.

That is not a failing of any particular company. A tool used by thousands of businesses in dozens of states cannot change its behavior every time one board in one state updates one rule. It is a reasonable trade, and it means your business waits.

## When the app is yours

When you own the app, the change is yours to make. You do not wait to see whether it makes someone else's list. You describe the change and apply it.

That is the real meaning of owning your software. It is not about technology. It is about who decides, and how quickly your tools can match what you are actually required to do.

And because AI writes the code now, making the change does not mean learning to program. The work is the same work as building any feature: saying clearly what should happen.

> From now on, a client cannot book this service until they have answered the new question on the intake form. If they answered it at an earlier visit, ask it again before each new appointment.

That kind of sentence, precise about what changes and when, is most of the job. I wrote more about writing them in [how to describe what you want built](/build/blog/describing-what-you-want-built).

## The honest trade-off: you are the one who checks

Owning the change also means owning the checking. Nobody else is testing your update before your clients see it. So when a rule changes:

1. **Read the change at the source,** not a summary of it, and note exactly what it asks.
2. **Decide what it means for your app.** A new form question? A booking rule? A record to keep? Sometimes it means nothing in the app at all.
3. **Describe the change and make it.**
4. **Test it as a client would.** Book the service. Fill in the form. Try the case the rule exists for, and the case it should not affect. I wrote more about checking without reading code in [testing an app you cannot read](/build/blog/testing-an-app-you-cannot-read).
5. **Keep a note of what changed and when.** If anyone ever asks when you started asking a question or keeping a record, you will know.

That is real responsibility, and it is worth saying plainly. If a rule change matters for client safety, the update needs the same care you would give the service itself.

## Records you already kept still matter

One more thing to think about with any change: records from before it. A new rule about what to record usually applies from now on, but the old records still need to be kept and readable for as long as your board requires. When you change a form, make sure the answers clients gave on the old version are still there and still make sense.

## Why this matters more over time

On the day you build your app, it fits your work perfectly. The real test comes later, when your work changes. A tool that cannot follow you turns every change into a workaround, and workarounds pile up. A tool you own can keep fitting for as long as you run the business.
